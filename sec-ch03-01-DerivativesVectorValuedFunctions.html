    Section 3.1 Derivatives of Vector-Valued Functions

    Note 3.1.1.

    Given a vector-valued function \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle f(t), g(t), h(t)\rangle\text{,}\) we learned that for a specific \(t\) value \(t_{0}, \vec{r}\left(t_{0}\right)\) tells you the location of a particle travelling along the graph of \(\vec{r}(t)\) at time \(t_{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    What if we want to know how fast and in what direction is the particle travelling at a given time? We can answer these questions using differentiation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.2. Theoretical Definition of the Derivative.

    Given a vector-valued function \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle f(t), g(t), h(t)\rangle\text{,}\) defined on some open interval, its derivative at \(t=t_{0}\) is defined as
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)=\lim _{\Delta t \rightarrow 0} \frac{\vec{r}\left(t_{0}+\Delta t\right)-\vec{r}\left(t_{0}\right)}{\Delta t} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.1.3.

    • Since \(\vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)\) is defined as a limit, it might not exist.
      🔗
      🔗
    • To evaluate a limit of a vector-valued function, you evaluate the limit in each component.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.4.

    Given the function \(\vec{r}(t)=\left\langle t^{3}-1, t+2\right\rangle\) find:
    🔗
    (a) \(\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) \(\vec{r}^{\prime}(1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    We use the definition of the derivative, evaluating the limit in each component:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}'(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\vec{r}(t+\Delta t)-\vec{r}(t)}{\Delta t} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\left\langle (t+\Delta t)^3-1, (t+\Delta t)+2 \right\rangle - \left\langle t^3-1, t+2 \right\rangle}{\Delta t} \end{equation*}
    In the first component, expanding \((t+\Delta t)^3 = t^3+3t^2\Delta t+3t\Delta t^2+\Delta t^3\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\left( (t+\Delta t)^3-1 \right)-\left( t^3-1 \right)}{\Delta t} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{3t^2\Delta t+3t\Delta t^2+\Delta t^3}{\Delta t}\\ = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \left( 3t^2+3t\Delta t+\Delta t^2 \right) = 3t^2 \end{gather*}
    In the second component:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\left( (t+\Delta t)+2 \right)-\left( t+2 \right)}{\Delta t} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta t} = 1 \end{equation*}
    Hence \(\vec{r}'(t) = \left\langle 3t^2, 1 \right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Evaluating the derivative from part (a) at \(t=1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}'(1) = \left\langle 3(1)^2, 1 \right\rangle = \langle 3,1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.1.5.

    In Calculus I, \(f^{\prime}(x)\) represents (geometrically) a limit of secant lines approaching a tangent line. Similarly, \(\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)\) represents (geometrically) a limit of “secant” vectors approaching a tangent vector.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.6. Computational Definition of the Derivative.

    Consider a vector-valued function \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle f(t), g(t), h(t)\rangle\text{.}\) If \(f, g\text{,}\) and \(h\) are all differentiable on the \(t\)-interval ( \(a, b\) ), then \(\vec{r}(t)\) is differentiable on the \(t\)-interval ( \(a, b\) ), and its derivative is given by the vector-valued function
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}^{\prime}(t)=\left\langle f^{\prime}(t), g^{\prime}(t), h^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.7.

    Let \(\vec{r}(t)=\left\langle\ln \left(\frac{2t^2}{\pi}-1\right), \frac{t^{3}}{3\pi}, \cos (t+3\pi)\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) On what interval is \(\vec{r}(t)\) differentiable?
    🔗
    (b) Compute \(\vec{r}^{\prime}(\pi)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    By the computational definition, \(\vec{r}(t)\) is differentiable wherever all three components are differentiable. The components \(\frac{t^3}{3\pi}\) and \(\cos(t+3\pi)\) are differentiable for every \(t\text{.}\) The component \(\ln\left( \frac{2t^2}{\pi}-1 \right)\) is defined and differentiable when the input of the logarithm is positive:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2t^2}{\pi}-1 \gt 0 \Rightarrow t^2 \gt \frac{\pi}{2} \Rightarrow t \lt -\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}} \ \text{ or } \ t \gt \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}} \end{equation*}
    Hence \(\vec{r}(t)\) is differentiable on the intervals \(\left( -\infty, -\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}} \right)\) and \(\left( \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}, \infty \right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Differentiating each component (using the single-variable chain rule in the first and third):
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}'(t) = \left\langle \frac{\frac{4t}{\pi}}{\frac{2t^2}{\pi}-1}, \frac{t^2}{\pi}, -\sin(t+3\pi) \right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{4t}{2t^2-\pi}, \frac{t^2}{\pi}, -\sin(t+3\pi) \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    Since \(\pi \gt \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\text{,}\) the function is differentiable at \(t=\pi\text{,}\) and we can evaluate:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}'(\pi) = \left\langle \frac{4\pi}{2\pi^2-\pi}, \frac{\pi^2}{\pi}, -\sin(4\pi) \right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{4}{2\pi-1}, \pi, 0 \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.1.8. Properties of Derivatives of Vector-Valued Functions.

    Let \(\vec{u}(t)\) and \(\vec{v}(t)\) be differentiable vector-valued functions and let \(f(t)\) be a differentiable scalar-valued function, all at the input \(t\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    1. Constant Rule:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{d t}(\mathrm{c})=0 \end{equation*}
      for any constant vector \(\vec{c}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Sum Rule:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{d t}(\vec{u}(t)+\vec{v}(t))=\vec{u}^{\prime}(t)+\vec{v}^{\prime}(t) \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Chain Rule:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{d t}[\vec{u}(f(t))]=\vec{u}^{\prime}(f(t)) f^{\prime}(t) \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Dot Product Rule:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{d t}(\vec{u}(t) \cdot \vec{v}(t))=\vec{u}^{\prime}(t) \cdot \vec{v}(t)+\vec{u}(t) \cdot \vec{v}^{\prime}(t) \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Cross Product Rule:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{d t}(\vec{u}(t) \times \vec{v}(t))=\vec{u}^{\prime}(t) \times \vec{v}(t)+\vec{u}(t) \times \vec{v}^{\prime}(t) \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.1.9. Notation for the Chain Rule:.

    • When evaluating a derivative of the form \(\frac{d}{d t}[\vec{u}(f(t))]\text{,}\) if \(f(t) \neq t\text{,}\) then \(\frac{d}{d t}[\vec{u}(f(t))] \neq \vec{u}^{\prime}(f(t))\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(f(t) \neq t\text{,}\) interpret \(\vec{u}^{\prime}(f(t))\) as “take the derivative of \(\vec{u}\) with respect to \(t\text{,}\) then replace all \(t\) ’s with \(f(t)\text{.}\)”
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.10.

    Given \(\vec{u}(t)= (t^{2}+3t+1 )\vec{i}-e^{2t} \vec{k}\text{,}\) compute \(\frac{d}{d t}\left[\vec{u}\left(t^{3}\right)\right]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We use the Chain Rule \(\frac{d}{dt}\left[ \vec{u}(f(t)) \right] = \vec{u}'(f(t))f'(t)\) with \(f(t) = t^3\text{.}\) First,
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}'(t) = (2t+3)\vec{i} - 2e^{2t}\vec{k} \end{equation*}
    Following the note above, \(\vec{u}'(t^3)\) means we replace all \(t\)’s in \(\vec{u}'(t)\) with \(t^3\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}'(t^3) = (2t^3+3)\vec{i} - 2e^{2t^3}\vec{k} \end{equation*}
    Since \(f'(t) = 3t^2\text{,}\) the Chain Rule gives
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dt}\left[ \vec{u}(t^3) \right] = \left( (2t^3+3)\vec{i} - 2e^{2t^3}\vec{k} \right) 3t^2 = (6t^5+9t^2)\vec{i} - 6t^2e^{2t^3}\vec{k} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.11.

    Given a curve \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle f(t), g(t), h(t)\rangle\text{,}\) the vector \(\vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)\) is called the tangent vector to the curve at \(t=t_{0}\text{,}\) and is usually plotted with starting point at \(\left(f\left(t_{0}\right), g\left(t_{0}\right), h\left(t_{0}\right)\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Below is the graph of \(\vec{r}(t)=\left\langle 3\cos (t), 5\cos\left(\frac{t}{2}\right), \sin (t)\right\rangle, 0 \leq t \leq 4\pi\text{,}\) with \(\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)\) plotted every \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) units of time.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.12.

    We define the unit tangent vector to the graph of \(\vec{r}(t)\) at \(t=t_{0}\) as
    \begin{equation*} \vec{T}\left(t_{0}\right)=\frac{\vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)}{\left|\vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)\right|}, \quad\left|\vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)\right| \neq 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.13.

    Given \(\vec{r}(t)=\left\langle\sqrt{t}, \ln (t)\right\rangle, t \gt 0\text{,}\) find:
    🔗
    (a) The tangent vector to the graph of \(\vec{r}(t)\) at the point \(\left(1, 0\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) The unit tangent vector to the graph of \(\vec{r}(t)\) for arbitrary \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    First we find the \(t\)-value corresponding to the point \((1,0)\text{:}\) we need \(\sqrt{t} = 1\) and \(\ln(t) = 0\text{,}\) and both equations give \(t=1\text{.}\) Differentiating componentwise:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}'(t) = \left\langle \frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}, \frac{1}{t} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    so the tangent vector at the point \((1,0)\) is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}'(1) = \left\langle \frac{1}{2}, 1 \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    For \(t \gt 0\) the magnitude of \(\vec{r}'(t)\) is
    \begin{equation*} |\vec{r}'(t)| = \sqrt{\left( \frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}} \right)^2+\left( \frac{1}{t} \right)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4t}+\frac{1}{t^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{t+4}{4t^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{t+4}}{2t} \end{equation*}
    Hence the unit tangent vector is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{T}(t) = \frac{\vec{r}'(t)}{|\vec{r}'(t)|} = \frac{2t}{\sqrt{t+4}}\left\langle \frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}, \frac{1}{t} \right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{t+4}}, \frac{2}{\sqrt{t+4}} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    As a check, \(|\vec{T}(t)|^2 = \frac{t}{t+4}+\frac{4}{t+4} = 1\text{,}\) so \(\vec{T}(t)\) is indeed a unit vector.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.1.14. Velocity and Acceleration Vectors.

    Given a differentiable curve \(\vec{r}(t)\text{,}\) the vector \(\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)\) has an important physical interpretation:
    🔗
    • \(\left|\vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)\right|\) gives the speed of a particle traveling on \(\vec{r}(t)\) at \(t=t_{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\frac{\vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)}{\left|\vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)\right|}\) gives the direction of motion of a particle traveling on \(\vec{r}(t)\) at \(t=t_{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.15. Velocity and Acceleration Vectors.

    Suppose \(\vec{r}(t)\) is a twice differentiable function representing the position of a particle at time \(t\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    • \(\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)\) is the velocity vector of the particle at time \(t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\vec{r}^{\prime \prime}(t)\) is the acceleration vector of the particle at time \(t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.16.

    Suppose that the position of a particle is given by \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle t^2, \sqrt{t}, \ln (t)\rangle\text{,}\) \(t \gt 0\text{.}\) Find:
    🔗
    (a) The velocity vector of the particle at \(t=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) The speed of the particle at \(t=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) The direction of motion of the particle at time \(t=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    Differentiating componentwise, the velocity vector at time \(t\) is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}'(t) = \left\langle 2t, \frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}, \frac{1}{t} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    so at \(t=1\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}'(1) = \left\langle 2, \frac{1}{2}, 1 \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector:
    \begin{equation*} |\vec{r}'(1)| = \sqrt{2^2+\left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^2+1^2} = \sqrt{\frac{16}{4}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{4}{4}} = \sqrt{\frac{21}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{21}}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 3.
    The direction of motion is the unit vector in the direction of the velocity vector:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\vec{r}'(1)}{|\vec{r}'(1)|} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{21}}\left\langle 2, \frac{1}{2}, 1 \right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{4}{\sqrt{21}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{21}}, \frac{2}{\sqrt{21}} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.17.

    Using the parametrization of the unit circle \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle\sin (t), \cos (t)\rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \leq t \leq 2 \pi\text{,}\) find all points on the unit circle at which its tangent vector is orthogonal to \(\vec{r}(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The tangent vector is \(\vec{r}'(t) = \langle \cos(t), -\sin(t) \rangle\text{.}\) Checking orthogonality with the dot product:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r}(t) \cdot \vec{r}'(t) = \sin(t)\cos(t)+\cos(t)\left( -\sin(t) \right) = 0 \end{equation*}
    The dot product equals 0 for every value of \(t\text{,}\) so the tangent vector is orthogonal to \(\vec{r}(t)\) at every point of the unit circle. This matches the geometric fact that a tangent line to a circle is always perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of tangency – and here \(\vec{r}(t)\) is exactly that radius.
