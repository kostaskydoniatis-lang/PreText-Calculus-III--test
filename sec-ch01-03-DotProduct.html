    Section 1.3 The Dot Product

    Definition 1.3.1. Dot Product.

    Let \(\vec{u}=\left\langle u_{1}, u_{2}\right\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}\right\rangle\) be two vectors in the \(x y\)-plane (or in \(x y z\)-space; just add an extra entry). Then the dot product of \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) denoted by \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}\text{,}\) is given by:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}=u_{1} v_{1}+u_{2} v_{2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If \(\vec{u} \neq 0\) and \(\vec{v} \neq 0\text{,}\) the dot product can also be written as
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}=|\vec{u}||\vec{v}| \cos (\theta)\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(0 \leq \theta \leq \pi\text{,}\) and \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.2.

    • The dot product of two vectors is a scalar (number), not a vector.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Even though formulas 1 and 2 are equivalent, formula 1 is more useful for computation, formula 2 is more useful for applications.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Formula 2 also works in 3D space. Any two nonzero vectors, not on the same line, define a unique plane (later we will see how to write an equation for this plane). The formula uses the angle between the vectors in this unique plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Formula 2 is undefined if \(\vec{u}\) or \(\vec{v}\) equal \(\vec{0}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.3.3.

    Any time we say “the angle between the vectors”, unless otherwise stated, we mean the angle that is between 0 and \(\pi\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.4.

    Compute the following:
    🔗
    1. \(\langle 0, 1\rangle \cdot\langle 1, 1\rangle\text{,}\) using formula 1 .
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\langle1, 1\rangle \cdot\langle 0, 1\rangle\text{,}\) using formula 2 (you may assume the angle between the vectors is \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) ).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    Formula 1 multiplies the corresponding components and adds the results:
    \begin{equation*} \langle 0,1 \rangle \cdot \langle 1,1 \rangle = 0(1)+1(1) = 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The magnitudes are \(|\langle 1,1 \rangle| = \sqrt{1^2+1^2} = \sqrt{2}\) and \(|\langle 0,1 \rangle| = \sqrt{0^2+1^2} = 1\text{,}\) so formula 2 gives
    \begin{equation*} \langle 1,1 \rangle \cdot \langle 0,1 \rangle = \sqrt{2} \cdot 1 \cdot \cos\left( \frac{\pi}{4} \right) = \sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = 1 \end{equation*}
    which agrees with the result of item 1: the two formulas are equivalent, and the dot product does not depend on the order of the vectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.5.

    Find the angle between \(\vec{u}=\langle 2,1,2\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\langle 3,-1,1\rangle\text{.}\) What happens if we use the dot product to find the angle between \(\vec{u}=\langle 1,0\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\langle 0,-1\rangle\) ?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since both vectors are nonzero, we can solve formula 2 for \(\cos(\theta)\text{,}\) computing the dot product with formula 1:
    \begin{gather*} \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 2(3)+1(-1)+2(1) = 7, \quad |\vec{u}| = \sqrt{4+1+4} = 3, \quad |\vec{v}| = \sqrt{9+1+1} = \sqrt{11}\\ \cos(\theta) = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{u}||\vec{v}|} = \frac{7}{3\sqrt{11}} \Rightarrow \theta = \arccos\left( \frac{7}{3\sqrt{11}} \right) \end{gather*}
    🔗
    For \(\vec{u}=\langle 1,0\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\langle 0,-1\rangle\) the dot product is \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 1(0)+0(-1) = 0\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \cos(\theta) = \frac{0}{1 \cdot 1} = 0 \Rightarrow \theta = \frac{\pi}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We get a right angle: even though \(\vec{v}\) points along the negative \(y\)-axis, the angle between the two vectors that lies between 0 and \(\pi\) is \(\frac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) so the vectors are perpendicular.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.6. Orthogonality.

    Two vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) in 2D or 3D are orthogonal if \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.7.

    Orthogonal is another word for perpendicular (as defined in geometry). In 3D, the vectors are perpendicular in the plane they define.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.8.

    Determine whether the vectors \(\vec{u}=\langle 5,-2\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\langle 1,5/2\rangle\) are perpendicular using the dot product.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute the dot product using formula 1:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 5(1)+(-2)\left( \frac{5}{2} \right) = 5-5 = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0\text{,}\) the vectors are orthogonal, i.e. perpendicular.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.3.9. Orthogonal Projections.

    The dot product allows us define projections. Think of projections as squishing (casting a shadow of) one vector into another.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.10. Orthogonal Projections.

    Let \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) be two vectors (in 2D or 3D), with \(\vec{v} \neq 0\text{.}\) The orthogonal projection (or simply “projection”) of \(\vec{u}\) into \(\vec{v}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}=\left(\frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v}}\right) \vec{v}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.11.

    • When we say that we are projecting \(\vec{u}\) into \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) we are actually projecting \(\vec{u}\) into the line containing \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • As seen in the pictures, \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) is a vector. Specifically, a scalar multiple of \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Important: As seen in the pictures, if the angle \(\theta\) between \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) satisfies \(0 \leq \theta \lt \frac{\pi}{2}, \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) is in the direction of \(\vec{v}\text{.}\) If \(\frac{\pi}{2} \lt \theta \leq \pi, \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) is in the direction opposite of \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • What happens if the angle between \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) is \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) or \(\vec{u}=\vec{0}\) ?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.12.

    Given \(\vec{u}=\langle1,2,3\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\langle -3,-2,-1\rangle\text{,}\) compute \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\text{.}\) Are \(\vec{v}\) and proj \(_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) parallel? Justify your answer.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute the two dot products in the projection formula:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 1(-3)+2(-2)+3(-1) = -10, \quad \vec{v} \cdot \vec{v} = (-3)^2+(-2)^2+(-1)^2 = 14 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u} = \left( \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v}} \right) \vec{v} = -\frac{10}{14}\langle -3,-2,-1 \rangle = -\frac{5}{7}\langle -3,-2,-1 \rangle = \left\langle \frac{15}{7}, \frac{10}{7}, \frac{5}{7} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Yes, they are parallel: \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u} = -\frac{5}{7}\vec{v}\) is a scalar multiple of \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) so the two vectors lie on the same line. Since the scalar \(-\frac{5}{7}\) is negative, \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) points in the direction opposite of \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.13.

    Given any nonzero vector \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) we have that
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v} \cdot \vec{v}=v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}=|\vec{v}|^{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We can use this fact to re-write the projection formula as follows:
    \begin{align*} \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u} \amp =\left(\frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v}}\right) \vec{v}\\ \amp =\left(\frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|^{2}}\right) \vec{v}\\ \amp =\left(\frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}\right) \frac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.14. Scalar Component in the Direction of a Vector.

    Let \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) be two vectors with \(\vec{v} \neq \vec{0}\text{.}\) The scalar component of \(\vec{u}\) in the direction of \(\vec{v}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}=\frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Moreover, we have that
    \begin{equation*} \left|\operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\right|=\left|\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\right|\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.15. The importance of the quantity scal \({ }_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\).

    • \(\left|\operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\right|\) is a shortcut to compute \(\left|\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\right|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • From Note 6, if \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} \gt 0\text{,}\) then \(\operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u} \gt 0\text{,}\) and hence \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) is in the direction of \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} \lt 0\text{,}\) then \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) is in the direction opposite of \(\vec{v}\) .
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.16.

    Given \(\vec{u}=\langle 4,3\rangle, \vec{v}=\langle 5,12\rangle\text{,}\) and \(\vec{w}=\langle-4,-3\rangle\text{,}\) find \(\operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) and \(\operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{w}\text{.}\) Moreover, determine whether \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) and \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{w}\) are in the direction of \(\vec{v}\) or opposite to it.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, \(|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{5^2+12^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{gather*} \operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u} = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|} = \frac{4(5)+3(12)}{13} = \frac{56}{13}\\ \operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{w} = \frac{\vec{w} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|} = \frac{-4(5)+(-3)(12)}{13} = -\frac{56}{13} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Since \(\operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u} = \frac{56}{13} \gt 0\text{,}\) the projection \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) is in the direction of \(\vec{v}\text{;}\) since \(\operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{w} = -\frac{56}{13} \lt 0\text{,}\) the projection \(\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{w}\) is in the direction opposite of \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) as the picture above shows. (Note that \(\vec{w} = -\vec{u}\text{,}\) which is why the two scalar components are negatives of each other.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.3.17. Summary.

    Given two vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) with \(\vec{v} \neq 0\text{;}\) and \(0 \leq \theta \leq \pi\) the angle between \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) then:
    🔗
    • If \(0 \leq \theta \lt \frac{\pi}{2}\) or \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} \gt 0 \Rightarrow \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) is in the direction of \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\frac{\pi}{2} \lt \theta \leq \pi\) or \(\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} \lt 0 \Rightarrow \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\) is in the direction opposite of \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\) or \(\vec{u}=0 \Rightarrow \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • A shortcut to find \(\left|\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\right|\) is to compute it as \(\left|\operatorname{scal}_{\vec{v}} \vec{u}\right|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
