    Section 1.4 The Cross Product

    Definition 1.4.1. The Cross Product Geometric Definition.

    Given two nonzero vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) in \(x y z\)-space, their cross product, denoted by \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\text{,}\) is a vector orthogonal to both \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) with length given by the formula
    \begin{equation*} |\vec{u} \times \vec{v}|=|\vec{u}||\vec{v}| \sin (\theta)\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(0 \leq \theta \leq \pi\) is the angle between \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.4.2. Direction of \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\).

    Given two nonzero vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) not on the same line, to find the direction of \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) :
    🔗
    1. Put \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) in standard position.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Let the fingers of your right hand curl from \(\vec{u}\) to \(\vec{v}\) along \(\theta\) (along the angle that is between 0 and \(\pi\) ).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The direction of your thumb as you curl in step 2 is the direction of \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We call this method to find the direction of \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) the right-hand rule.
    🔗

    Example 1.4.3.

    Determine which of the following cross product vectors \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) point in the positive \(z\)-direction.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In each of the figures (a)–(d) above, both vectors lie in the \(xy\)-plane, so \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) points either in the positive or in the negative \(z\)-direction. By the right-hand rule, it points in the positive \(z\)-direction exactly when curling from \(\vec{u}\) to \(\vec{v}\) along the angle \(\theta\) between them (\(0 \leq \theta \leq \pi\)) is a counterclockwise rotation.
    🔗
    In (a) the counterclockwise rotation from \(\vec{u}\) to \(\vec{v}\) is \(\frac{3\pi}{4} \lt \pi\text{,}\) so this rotation is along the angle between the vectors, and \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) points in the positive \(z\)-direction. The same happens in (d), where the counterclockwise rotation is \(\frac{5\pi}{12} \lt \pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In (b) the counterclockwise rotation from \(\vec{u}\) to \(\vec{v}\) is \(\frac{13\pi}{12} \gt \pi\text{,}\) so the angle between the vectors is \(2\pi-\frac{13\pi}{12} = \frac{11\pi}{12}\text{,}\) and curling from \(\vec{u}\) to \(\vec{v}\) along this angle is a clockwise rotation: \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) points in the negative \(z\)-direction. The same happens in (c), where the angle between the vectors is \(2\pi-\frac{5\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}\text{,}\) again traversed clockwise.
    🔗
    Hence only the cross products in (a) and (d) point in the positive \(z\)-direction.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.4.4.

    • If \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}=\vec{0}\) (if \(\theta=0\) or \(\theta=\pi\) ), the direction of \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) is undefined.
      🔗
      🔗
    • For nonzero \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) the cross product is not commutative. In fact, \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}=\) \(-(\vec{v} \times \vec{u})\) (they have the same length, but point in opposite directions).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.5.

    Given the figure below, find:
    🔗
    1. The direction of \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The magnitude of \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The vector \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    From the figure, \(\vec{u} = \langle 0,2,0 \rangle\) lies on the positive \(y\)-axis and \(\vec{v} = \langle 0,0,4 \rangle\) lies on the positive \(z\)-axis. Curling the fingers of the right hand from the positive \(y\)-axis towards the positive \(z\)-axis, the thumb points in the positive \(x\)-direction. Hence \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) points in the positive \(x\)-direction (the direction of \(\vec{i}\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The angle between the positive \(y\)-axis and the positive \(z\)-axis is \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} |\vec{u} \times \vec{v}| = |\vec{u}||\vec{v}|\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 1 = 8 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 3.
    Combining the two previous parts, \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) is the vector of length 8 pointing in the positive \(x\)-direction:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} \times \vec{v} = 8\vec{i} = \langle 8,0,0 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.4.6. Cross Products Computational Definition.

    Given two vectors \(\vec{u}=\left\langle u_{1}, u_{2}, u_{3}\right\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}, v_{3}\right\rangle\) (they could be the zero vector) in \(x y z\)-space,
    \begin{align*} \vec{u} \times \vec{v} \amp =\left|\begin{array}{ccc} \vec{i} \amp \vec{j} \amp \vec{k} \\ u_{1} \amp u_{2} \amp u_{3} \\ v_{1} \amp v_{2} \amp v_{3} \end{array}\right|=\left|\begin{array}{ll} u_{2} \amp u_{3} \\ v_{2} \amp v_{3} \end{array}\right| \vec{i}-\left|\begin{array}{ll} u_{1} \amp u_{3} \\ v_{1} \amp v_{3} \end{array}\right| \vec{j}+\left|\begin{array}{ll} u_{1} \amp u_{2} \\ v_{1} \amp v_{2} \end{array}\right| \vec{k}\\ \amp =\left(u_{2} v_{3}-u_{3} v_{2}\right) \vec{i}-\left(u_{1} v_{3}-u_{3} v_{1}\right) \vec{j}+\left(u_{1} v_{2}-u_{2} v_{1}\right) \vec{k}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.7.

    Compute \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\) given that \(\vec{u}=\langle 1,2,3\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\langle 2,1,3\rangle\text{.}\) What is \(\vec{v} \times \vec{u}\) ?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the computational definition:
    \begin{gather*} \vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \left| \begin{matrix} \vec{i} \amp \vec{j} \amp \vec{k} \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{matrix}\right|\\ = \left| \begin{matrix} 2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 3 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{i} - \left| \begin{matrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 3 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{j} + \left| \begin{matrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{k}\\ = \left( 2(3)-3(1) \right)\vec{i} - \left( 1(3)-3(2) \right)\vec{j} + \left( 1(1)-2(2) \right)\vec{k}\\ = 3\vec{i}+3\vec{j}-3\vec{k} = \langle 3,3,-3 \rangle \end{gather*}
    🔗
    For the second question there is no need to compute another determinant: since \(\vec{v} \times \vec{u} = -(\vec{u} \times \vec{v})\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v} \times \vec{u} = -\langle 3,3,-3 \rangle = \langle -3,-3,3 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.4.8. The Cross Product and Parallel Vectors.

    Given two vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) if \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v}=\vec{0}, \vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) are parallel (lie on the same line).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.9.

    Determine whether the vectors \(\vec{u}=\langle -3,6,0\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\langle1,-2,0\rangle\) lie on the same line.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute the cross product:
    \begin{gather*} \vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \left| \begin{matrix} \vec{i} \amp \vec{j} \amp \vec{k} \\ -3 \amp 6 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp -2 \amp 0 \end{matrix}\right|\\ = \left| \begin{matrix} 6 \amp 0 \\ -2 \amp 0 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{i} - \left| \begin{matrix} -3 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{j} + \left| \begin{matrix} -3 \amp 6 \\ 1 \amp -2 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{k}\\ = \left( 6(0)-0(-2) \right)\vec{i} - \left( (-3)(0)-0(1) \right)\vec{j} + \left( (-3)(-2)-6(1) \right)\vec{k}\\ = 0\vec{i}-0\vec{j}+(6-6)\vec{k} = \vec{0} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Since \(\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \vec{0}\text{,}\) the vectors are parallel, and hence they lie on the same line.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.4.10.

    Another way to determine whether two vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) are parallel is to check whether there exists a scalar \(c\) such that \(c \vec{u}=\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.11.

    Determine whether \(\vec{u}=\langle 1,2,1\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}=\langle 2,-1,2\rangle\) are parallel.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Suppose there were a scalar \(c\) such that \(c\vec{u} = \vec{v}\text{.}\) Comparing components:
    \begin{equation*} c\cdot 1 = 2 \Rightarrow c = 2, \quad c \cdot 2 = -1 \Rightarrow c = -\frac{1}{2}, \quad c \cdot 1 = 2 \Rightarrow c = 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The second component requires a different scalar than the other two, so no such \(c\) exists and the vectors are not parallel. We can confirm this with the cross product:
    \begin{gather*} \vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \left| \begin{matrix} \vec{i} \amp \vec{j} \amp \vec{k} \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp -1 \amp 2 \end{matrix}\right|\\ = \left| \begin{matrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 2 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{i} - \left| \begin{matrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 2 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{j} + \left| \begin{matrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp -1 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{k}\\ = \left( 2(2)-1(-1) \right)\vec{i} - \left( 1(2)-1(2) \right)\vec{j} + \left( 1(-1)-2(2) \right)\vec{k} = \langle 5,0,-5 \rangle \neq \vec{0} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.4.12. The Cross Product and Parallelograms.

    Given two nonzero vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v},|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}|\) is the area of the parallelogram defined by \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.13.

    Find the area of the triangle with vertices given by the points \(A(1,2,3), B(2,3,4), C(4,3,2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The triangle is half of the parallelogram defined by the vectors \(\vec{AB}\) and \(\vec{AC}\text{,}\) so its area is \(\frac{1}{2}\left| \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} \right|\text{.}\) In standard position:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{AB} = \langle 2-1, 3-2, 4-3 \rangle = \langle 1,1,1 \rangle, \quad \vec{AC} = \langle 4-1, 3-2, 2-3 \rangle = \langle 3,1,-1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Their cross product is
    \begin{gather*} \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = \left| \begin{matrix} \vec{i} \amp \vec{j} \amp \vec{k} \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 3 \amp 1 \amp -1 \end{matrix}\right|\\ = \left| \begin{matrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{i} - \left| \begin{matrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 3 \amp -1 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{j} + \left| \begin{matrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 3 \amp 1 \end{matrix}\right|\vec{k}\\ = \left( 1(-1)-1(1) \right)\vec{i} - \left( 1(-1)-1(3) \right)\vec{j} + \left( 1(1)-1(3) \right)\vec{k} = \langle -2,4,-2 \rangle \end{gather*}
    🔗
    with magnitude
    \begin{equation*} \left| \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} \right| = \sqrt{(-2)^2+4^2+(-2)^2} = \sqrt{24} = 2\sqrt{6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence the area of the triangle is \(\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2\sqrt{6} = \sqrt{6}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.4.14.

    Properties of the Cross Product
    🔗
    Given two vectors \(\vec{u}\) and \(\vec{v}\) in \(x y z\)-space and \(a, b\) real numbers, then
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \vec{u} \times \vec{v}=-(\vec{v} \times \vec{u})\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle (a \vec{u}) \times(b \vec{v})=a b(\vec{u} \times \vec{v})\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \vec{u} \times(\vec{v}+\vec{w})=(\vec{u} \times \vec{v})+(\vec{u} \times \vec{w})\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle (\vec{u}+\vec{v}) \times \vec{w}=(\vec{u} \times \vec{w})+(\vec{v} \times \vec{w})\)
      🔗
      🔗
