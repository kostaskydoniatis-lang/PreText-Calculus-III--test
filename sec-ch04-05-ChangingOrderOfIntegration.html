    Section 4.5 Changing Order of Integration

    Note 4.5.1.

    As with double integrals over rectangular regions, under some continuity assumptions, we may change the order of integration.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 4.5.2. Fubini’s Theorem (General Regions).

    Let \(f(x, y)\) be continuous on a region \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. If \(R\) is defined by \(a \leq x \leq b, g_{1}(x) \leq y \leq h_{1}(x)\text{,}\) with \(g_{1}\) and \(h_{1}\) continuous on \([a, b]\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} \iint_{R} f(x, y) d A=\int_{a}^{b} \int_{g_{1}(x)}^{h_{1}(x)} f(x, y) d y d x \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(R\) is defined by \(c \leq y \leq d, g_{2}(y) \leq y \leq h_{2}(y)\text{,}\) with \(g_{2}\) and \(h_{2}\) continuous on \([c, d]\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} \iint_{R} f(x, y) d A=\int_{c}^{d} \int_{g_{2}(y)}^{h_{2}(y)} f(x, y) d x d y \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.5.3.

    Changing the order of integration over regions that are not rectangular requires more work. An easy way to do it is by drawing a sketch of the region of integration.
    🔗
    This means that we should be able to draw the region of integration just by looking at the bounds of integration from the iterated integrals.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.5.4.

    Given the integral \(\int_{-1}^{1} \int_{ x^{2}}^{2- x^2} 2xy d y d x\) :
    🔗
    (a) Draw a sketch of the region of integration.
    🔗
    (b) Use your sketch to change the order of integration.
    🔗
    (c) Evaluate the double integral.
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The bounds \(-1\leq x \leq 1\) and \(x^2\leq y \leq 2-x^2\) describe the region between the upward-opening parabola \(y=x^2\) and the downward-opening parabola \(y=2-x^2\text{.}\) They intersect when \(x^2=2-x^2 \Rightarrow x^2=1 \Rightarrow x=\pm 1\text{,}\) confirming the outer bounds. The region lies between the two parabolas for \(-1\leq x\leq 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    To integrate in the order \(dx\,dy\text{,}\) we need to express \(x\) as a function of \(y\text{.}\) From the two parabolas: \(y=x^2\) gives \(x=\pm\sqrt{y}\text{,}\) and \(y=2-x^2\) gives \(x=\pm\sqrt{2-y}\text{.}\) The region has \(y\) ranging from the bottom of the lower parabola to the top of the upper one. The lower parabola has its vertex at \((0,0)\) and the upper at \((0,2)\text{;}\) they meet at \(y=1\text{.}\) For \(0\leq y\leq 1\) the horizontal cross-section is bounded on the left and right by \(x=-\sqrt{y}\) and \(x=\sqrt{y}\text{.}\) For \(1\leq y\leq 2\) the cross-section is bounded by \(x=-\sqrt{2-y}\) and \(x=\sqrt{2-y}\text{.}\) Hence, the integral with reversed order is
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1\int_{-\sqrt{y}}^{\sqrt{y}} 2xy\, dx\, dy + \int_1^2\int_{-\sqrt{2-y}}^{\sqrt{2-y}} 2xy\, dx\, dy \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 3.
    Evaluating using the original order is more straightforward here.
    \begin{gather*} \int_{-1}^{1} \int_{x^{2}}^{2-x^2} 2xy\, dy\, dx = \int_{-1}^{1} \left. xy^2 \right|_{x^2}^{2-x^2} dx = \int_{-1}^{1} x\left[(2-x^2)^2 - x^4\right] dx\\ = \int_{-1}^{1} x\left[4 - 4x^2 + x^4 - x^4\right] dx = \int_{-1}^{1} x(4-4x^2)\, dx\\ = \int_{-1}^{1} 4x - 4x^3\, dx = \left. 2x^2 - x^4 \right|_{-1}^{1} = (2-1)-(2-1) = 0 \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.5.5.

    Given the integral \(\int_{-1}^{0} \int_{x}^{-x^2} 2x+2y\, d y\, d x\) :
    🔗
    (a) Draw a sketch of the region of integration.
    🔗
    (b) Use your sketch to change the order of integration.
    🔗
    (c) Evaluate the double integral.
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The bounds \(-1\leq x\leq 0\) and \(x\leq y\leq -x^2\) describe the region above the line \(y=x\) and below the downward-opening parabola \(y=-x^2\text{.}\) They intersect when \(x=-x^2 \Rightarrow x^2+x=0 \Rightarrow x(x+1)=0\text{,}\) giving \(x=0\) and \(x=-1\text{,}\) confirming the outer bounds. For \(-1\leq x\leq 0\) the line lies below the parabola.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    To integrate in the order \(dx\,dy\text{,}\) we express \(x\) as a function of \(y\text{.}\) From \(y=-x^2\) we get \(x=-\sqrt{-y}\) (taking the negative square root since \(x\leq 0\)), so the parabola gives the left boundary. From \(y=x\) we get \(x=y\text{,}\) which gives the right boundary. The variable \(y\) ranges from the bottom intersection point to the top. At \((-1,-1)\) both curves meet, giving \(y=-1\) as the lower bound. The parabola reaches \(y=0\) at \(x=0\text{,}\) and the line also passes through \((0,0)\text{,}\) giving \(y=0\) as the upper bound. Hence the reversed integral is
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-1}^{0}\int_{-\sqrt{-y}}^{y} 2x+2y\, dx\, dy \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 3.
    Evaluating using the original order is more straightforward here.
    \begin{gather*} \int_{-1}^{0}\int_{x}^{-x^2} 2x+2y\, dy\, dx = \int_{-1}^{0} \left. 2xy + y^2 \right|_{x}^{-x^2} dx\\ = \int_{-1}^{0} \left[2x(-x^2)+(-x^2)^2\right] - \left[2x(x)+x^2\right] dx\\ = \int_{-1}^{0} \left[-2x^3 + x^4\right] - \left[2x^2+x^2\right] dx\\ = \int_{-1}^{0} x^4 - 2x^3 - 3x^2\, dx\\ = \left. \frac{x^5}{5} - \frac{x^4}{2} - x^3 \right|_{-1}^{0} = 0 - \left(-\frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{2} + 1\right) = \frac{1}{5}+\frac{1}{2}-1 = -\frac{3}{10} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.5.6. Volume Between Surfaces and Area.

    Just like in Calculus II we were able to find area bounded between functions, we can similarly find the volume bounded between surfaces.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.5.7. Volume Between Surfaces.

    Suppose that \(f(x, y)\) and \(g(x, y)\) are continuous on a region \(R\) and that \(f(x, y) \geq g(x, y)\) on \(R\text{.}\) Then the volume bounded by \(f(x, y)\) and \(g(x, y)\) on \(R\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} V=\iint_{R}(f(x, y)-g(x, y)) d A \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.5.8.

    Set up (but do not evaluate) an iterated integral that gives the volume of the solid in the first octant ( \(x, y, z \geq 0\) ) bounded by the surfaces \(z=xy+5\text{,}\) \(z=2x+2y\) above the circle \(x^{2}+y^2=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The volume bounded by the given surfaces
    🔗
    Solution.
    First we determine which surface is on top. The region of integration \(R\) is the part of the unit disk \(x^2+y^2\leq 1\) in the first quadrant, i.e. \(1\geq x\geq 0\) and \(1\geq y\geq 0\text{.}\) This implies that \(\geq xy +5 \geq 5 \gt 4 \geq 2x+2y\)
    🔗
    For a fixed \(x\in[0,1]\text{,}\) \(y\) ranges from \(0\) to \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\text{.}\) Hence the volume is
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^1\int_0^{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \left[(xy+5)-(2x+2y)\right] dy\, dx = \int_0^1\int_0^{\sqrt{1-x^2}} xy - 2x - 2y + 5\, dy\, dx \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.5.9. Finding Area Using Double Integrals.

    Suppose you have a region \(R\) in the \(x y\)-plane, you can find the area of \(R\) by using the double integral
    \begin{equation*} \iint_{R} d A \end{equation*}
    🔗
    That is, integrating the function \(f(x, y)=1\) over \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.5.10.

    Explain geometrically why the area of \(R\) is given by \(\iint_{R} d A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Recall that \(\iint_R f(x,y)\, dA\) gives the net volume of the solid bounded by the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) and the \(xy\)-plane, over the region \(R\text{.}\) Choosing \(f(x,y)=1\) means the surface is the horizontal plane \(z=1\text{,}\) which sits exactly one unit above the \(xy\)-plane at every point of \(R\text{.}\) The solid bounded between \(z=0\) and \(z=1\) over \(R\) is therefore a slab of uniform height 1. Its volume equals base area times height, that is \(\text{Area}(R)\times 1 = \text{Area}(R)\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R 1\, dA = \text{Area}(R)\text{.} \end{equation*}
