    Section 4.8 Triple Integrals in Cartesian Coordinates

    Note 4.8.1.

    We can use double integrals to calculate the net volume bounded by a surface(2-dimensional object, embedded in 3-dimensional space) \(f(x, y)\) and the \(x y\)-plane on a region \(R\) in the \(x y\)-plane.
    🔗
    Now, given a function \(f(x, y, z)\) (3-dimensional object, embedded in 4-dimensional space) and a region \(D\) in its domain (xyz-space), we will derive a way to calculate the 4-dimensional net hyper-volume bounded by \(f(x, y, z)\) and \(x y z\)-space on \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In summary, we will partition the 4 -dimensional net volume into 4 -dimensional net volumes of 4D rectangular prisms. The process is as follows:
    🔗
    1. Partition the region of integration \(D\) in \(x y z\)-space into rectangular prisms.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Denote the volume of the \(k\)-th rectangular prism as \(\Delta V_{k}=\Delta z_{k} \Delta y_{k} \Delta x_{k}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      • \(\Delta V_{k}\) is the volume of the “base” of the \(k\)-th 4 -dimensional prism.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Pick a point ( \(x_{k}, y_{k}, z_{k}\) ) in the \(k\)-th rectangular prism and evaluate \(f\left(x_{k}, y_{k}, z_{k}\right)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      • \(f\left(x_{k}, y_{k}, z_{k}\right)\) is the “height” of the \(k\)-th 4 -dimensional prism.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Now, we can approximate the 4 -dimensional net volume bounded by \(f(x, y, z)\) and \(x y z\)-space, restricted to \(D\text{,}\) by adding up the 4 -dimensional net volumes of the prisms. That is,
    \begin{equation*} V \approx \sum_{k=1}^{n} f\left(x_{k}, y_{k}, z_{k}\right) \Delta V_{k} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} V=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} f\left(x_{k}, y_{k}, z_{k}\right) \Delta V_{k}=\iiint_{D} f(x, y, z) d V \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We call this net volume the triple integral of \(f(x, y, z)\) over \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Note 4.8.2.

    We will evaluate triple integrals by reducing the problem to a double integral, with an extra step.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.8.3. Evaluating Triple Integrals.

    To integrate a continuous function \(f(x, y, z)\) over a 3 -dimensional region \(D\text{,}\) rewrite the integral as follows:
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_{D} f(x, y, z) d V=\iint_{R}\left(\int_{G(x, y)}^{H(x, y)} f(x, y, z) d z\right) d A \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Here, \(R\) is the projection of the 3 -dimensional region \(D\) into the \(x y\) plane.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.8.4.

    To find the bounds of integration for \(\iiint_{D} f(x, y, z) d V\) :
    🔗
    1. Draw vertical lines parallel to the \(z\)-axis (from the negative \(z\) to the positive \(z\) direction).
      🔗
      • Wherever these lines enter the region \(D\text{,}\) this is the lower bound of integration \(G(x, y)\) with respect to \(z\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Wherever these lines exit the region \(D\text{,}\) this is the upper bound of integration \(H(x, y)\) with respect to \(z\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Project (cast a shadow) of the 3 -dimensional region \(D\) into the \(x y\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Set up bounds for the other two integrals as you would do any other double integral.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. In the end, your triple integral should have the form (depending on order of integration):
      \begin{equation*} \iiint_{D} f(x, y, z) d V=\int_{a}^{b} \int_{g(x)}^{h(x)} \int_{G(x, y)}^{H(x, y)} f(x, y, z) d z d y d x \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.8.5. Volume as a Triple Integral.

    In order to compute the volume of a 3-dimensional solid \(D\text{,}\) we can use the triple integral
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_{D} 1 d V \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.8.6.

    Find the mass of a solid box bounded by the planes \(x=0\) and \(x=3\text{,}\) \(y=0\) and \(y=1, z=0\) and \(z=2\text{.}\) The density (mass per unit volume) of the box is given by \(\rho(x, y, z)=x^2ze^{xyz}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The box in \(x y z\)-space
    🔗
    Solution.
    The Mass can be calculated by sectioning the volume into small, manageable shapes, usually cubes and multiplying the volume of each cube with its density. Since the density is a non constant function, instead of a discrete sum we use the integral, which after all, is a the limit of a sum with infinite terms. Notice that since the bounds of integration for all variables are constants, one can freely choose the order of integration it suits them. Since it appears that integrating with respect to \(y\) first is more manageable the integral is set up this way.
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \text{Mass} = \iiint_D \rho (x,y,z)dV = \int_0^3\int_0^2\int_0^1 x^2ze^{xyz} dy dz dx =\\ u=xyz,\quad du = xz dy, \quad y= 0 \Rightarrow u = 0, \quad y =1 \Rightarrow u =xz\\ \int_0^3\int_0^2\int_0^{xz} xe^u du dz dx = \int_0^3\int_0^2\left . xe^u\right |_0^{xz} dy dz = \int_0^3\int_0^2 xe^{xz}-x dzdx =\\ \int_0^3\int_0^2 xe^{xz} dzdx -\int_0^3\int_0^2 xdzdx = \int_0^3\int_0^2 xe^{xz} dzdx -\int_0^3 2x dz = \int_0^3\int_0^2 xe^{xz} dzdx - 9 =\\ w=xz,\quad dw = x dz, \quad z= 0 \Rightarrow w = 0, \quad z =2 \Rightarrow w =2x\\ \int_0^3 \int_0^{2x} e^w dw -9 dx = \int_0^3 \left . e^w \right |_0^{2x} dz -9 =\int_0^3 e^{2x}- 1 dx -9 = \left . \frac{1}{2} e^{2x} -x \right |_0^3 - 9 =\\ \frac{1}{2}e^6 - \frac{1}{2} -3+0 -9 = \frac{1}{2}e^6-\frac{25}{2}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.8.7.

    Use a triple integral to find the volume of the solid in the first octant ( \(x, y, z \geq 0\) ) bounded by the plane \(x+2 y+ z=4\) and the coordinate planes.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The coordinate plane equations are \(z=0\text{,}\) \(x=0\text{,}\) and \(y=0\text{.}\) The plane \(x+2 y+ z=4\) or \(z = 4- 2y-x\) and \(z=0\) intersect at the line \(4-x-2y= 0\) of the \(xy\)-plane. For the intercepts of this line we have: \(x= 0 \Rightarrow 4 -2y= 0\Rightarrow (0,2)\) and \(y=0 \Rightarrow 4-x = 0 \Rightarrow (4,0)\text{.}\) From those points we see that the line segment we are interested at is in the first quadrant (the finite portion of this line). To see whether the plane \(z = 4- 2y-x\) is above or below the \(z=0\text{,}\) we need a point in the triangle defined by the \(x\)-axis, the \(y\)-axis, and the previous line segment. Chose a value of \(x\) within the bounds of the triangle, say \(x=2\text{.}\) Now to be within the triangle, this point has to have \(y\) coordinate less than the corresponding one of the line \(4-2y-x = 0\) \(x=2\) and \(0 = 4- 2y-x\) gives the point \((2,0.5)\text{.}\) For these \(x\) and \(y\) coordinates the \(z\) coordinate of the plane \(z = 4- 2y-x\) is \(z=4 -2 \cdot 0.5 - 2 = 4-3 =1 \gt 0\) and hence our plane is above the plane \(z=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence the bounds of integration with respect to \(z\) are \(0 \) as the lower one and \(4-2y-x \) as the upper one. Since we now that our line segment is is in the first quadrant we can solve \(4-x-2y =0\) for \(y\) and acquire the lower bound \(0\) and the upper bound \(2-\frac{x}{2}\) for \(y\text{.}\) Lastly for \(x\) we have \(0\) and \(4\) from the intercept of the line and the \(x\)-axis, calculated above.
    \begin{gather*} \text{Volume }= \int_0^4 \int_0^{2-\frac{x}{2}}\int_0^{4-x-2y} 1 dz dy dz = \int_0^4 \int_0^{2-\frac{x}{2}} 4 -x -2y dy dx = \int_0^4 \left . 4y -xy -y^2 \right |_0^{2-\frac{x}{2}}\\ \int_0^4 4(2-\frac{x}{2}) -x(2-\frac{x}{2}) -(2-\frac{x}{2})^2 dx = \int_0^4 4 -2x +\frac{x^2}{4} dx = \left . 4x-x^2+\frac{x^3}{12}\right |_0^4 = \frac{16}{3} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.8.8.

    Set up (but do not evaluate) a triple integral to find the volume of the solid between the sphere \(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=5\) and the cone \(z^{2}-4x^{2}-4y^{2}=0\text{,}\) for \(z \geq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    Since we have \(z\geq 0\) the shape is above the \(xy\)-plane. The intersection of the two surfaces is given by
    \begin{gather*} \left. \begin{matrix} x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\amp = \amp 5 \\ z^{2}-4x^{2}-4y^{2}\amp = \amp 0 \end{matrix} \right \} \Rightarrow \left. \begin{matrix} z^{2}\amp = \amp 5 - y^2 -x^2\\ z^{2}\amp = \amp 4x^{2}+4y^{2} \end{matrix} \right \} \Rightarrow 5 - y^2 -x^2 = 4x^{2}+4y^{2} \Rightarrow\\ 5x^2+5y^2 = 5 \Rightarrow x^2+y^2=1\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Hence the region of the \(xy\)-plane that the integral will be over is the unit disk centred at the origin. Similarly past examples we acquire the bounds \(-1\leq x \leq 1\) and \(-\sqrt(1-x^ 2)\leq y \leq \sqrt{1-x^2}\text{.}\) The last thing to be determined is if the cone is above the sphere or the other way around.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Using the coordinates \(x=0\) and \(y=0\) on the sphere \(0^2+0^2+z^2 = 5\overset{z\geq 0}{\Rightarrow} z= \sqrt 5\) while for the cone \(z^2-0^2-0^2 = 0 \Rightarrow z= 0\text{,}\) hence the sphere is above the cone. This part of the sphere can be expressed as \(z=\sqrt{5-X^2-Y^2}\) and this part of the cone as \(z=2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \text{Volume } =\int_{-1}^1\int_{-\sqrt{1-x^2}}^{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\int_{2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}^{\sqrt{5-x^2-y^2}} 1 dz d y dz \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.8.9. Changing Order of Integration.

    When integrating over a 3D region \(D\text{,}\) you may change the order of integration of the iterated integrals (including the first/innermost integral). The three things to remember are:
    🔗
    1. To find the bounds of integration for the inner integral, draw lines parallel to the axis of the variable of integration.
      🔗
      • For example, if integrating in the order \(d x d z d y\text{,}\) you would draw lines parallel to the \(x\)-axis.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Once you are done with the inner integral, you project \(D\) into the plane containing the other two variables.
      🔗
      • For example, if using the order \(d x d z d y\text{,}\) you would project \(D\) into the \(y z\)-plane.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The bounds of integration for each integral (working inside to outside) are functions of the next variables of integration.
      🔗
      • For example, when using the order \(d x d y d z\text{,}\) you would get
        \begin{equation*} \iiint_{D} f(x, y, z) d V=\int_{m}^{n} \int_{g(z)}^{h(z)} \int_{G(y, z)}^{H(y, z)} f(x, y, z) d x d y d z \end{equation*}
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.8.10.

    Set up (but do not evaluate) a triple integral to find the volume of the solid bounded by \(x=2-y^{2}\) and \(x= y^{2}+2 z^{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The region of integration \(D\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(x\) was provided as a function of \(y\) and \(z\text{,}\) the triple integral will be set up with the innermost integration being with respect to \(x\text{.}\) For the intersect of the above surface one has \(2-y^2= 2y^2+2^2 \Rightarrow y^2+z^2 = 1\text{.}\) Hence the region of the \(yz\)-plane that the integral will be over is the unit disk centred at the origin. Similarly past examples we acquire the bounds \(-1\leq z \leq 1\) and \(-\sqrt(1-z^ 2)\leq z \leq \sqrt{1-z^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using the coordinates \(y=0\) and \(z= 0\) one sees that \(x=2-y^2\) is above \(x=y^2+2z^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \text{Volume } =\int_{-1}^1\int_{-\sqrt{1-z^2}}^{\sqrt{1-z^2}}\int_{y^2+2z^2}^{2-y^2} 1 dx d y dz \end{equation*}
