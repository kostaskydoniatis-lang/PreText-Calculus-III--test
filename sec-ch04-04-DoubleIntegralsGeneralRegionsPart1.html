    Section 4.4 Double Integrals in Cartesian Coordinates

    Note 4.4.1.

    In this section we will do the same thing we did in the previous: We will find the net volume bounded by a surface \(f(x, y)\) and the \(x y\)-plane, restricted to a region \(R\) in its domain.
    🔗
    🔗
    However, we will now consider regions in the plane whose upper and lower bounds are given by curves (rather than straight lines as with rectangles), as shown below:
    🔗

    Note 4.4.2.

    The process to find the net volume is exactly the same as before. We still partition the region \(R\) into rectangles, although at first they won’t fit inside very nicely.
    🔗
    🔗
    As we increase the number of rectangles, we will get a better fit. When we take the number of rectangles to approach infinity, we theoretically cover the whole region \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since the height of each rectangular prism is still given by \(f\left(x_{k}, y_{k}\right)\) (where \(\left(x_{k}, y_{k}\right)\) is a point in the \(k\)-th rectangle) and the area of each rectangle is still \(A_{k}=\Delta x_{k} \Delta y_{k}\text{,}\) we get the same formula for the net volume:
    \begin{equation*} V=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} f\left(x_{k}, y_{k}\right) \Delta A_{k}=\iint_{R} f(x, y) d A \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Note 4.4.3. Evaluating Double Integrals over General Regions.

    As before, we will partition the net volume into cross sections, with net area \(A(x)\text{,}\) parallel to the \(y\)-axis (keep \(x\) as a constant); the difference is the upper and lower bounds of the region \(R\) are now functions \(g(x)\) and \(h(x)\) (instead of constants)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.4.

    Suppose we want to integrate the function \(f(x, y)=3 x+ y\) on the region \(R\) bounded above by \(y=x\) and below by \(y=\frac{1}{2} x\text{,}\) for \(0 \leq x \leq 2\text{,}\) as shown below. Find the net area of the cross section corresponding to \(x=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    At \(x=1\) the region \(R\) has \(y\) ranging from the lower bound \(y=\frac{1}{2}x\) to the upper bound \(y=x\text{.}\) Substituting \(x=1\) gives \(y\) ranging from \(\frac{1}{2}\) to \(1\text{.}\) The net area of the cross section is the integral of \(f(1, y) = 3 + y\) with respect to \(y\) over this interval:
    \begin{equation*} A(1) = \int_{1/2}^{1} (3 + y)\, dy = \left. 3y + \frac{y^2}{2} \right|_{1/2}^{1} = \left(3 + \frac{1}{2}\right) - \left(\frac{3}{2} + \frac{1}{8}\right) = \frac{7}{2} - \frac{13}{8} = \frac{28}{8} - \frac{13}{8} = \frac{15}{8}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    More generally, to find the net area of an arbitrary cross section, we integrate \(f(x, y)\) with respect to \(y\) with the bounds of integration given by the upper and lower bounds of \(R\text{.}\) That is,
    \begin{equation*} A(x)=\int_{g(x)}^{h(x)} f(x, y) d y \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Consequently, the net volume of the whole region is given by
    \begin{equation*} V=\int_{a}^{b} A(x) d x \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Remark 4.4.5. Evaluating Double Integrals as Iterated Integrals.

    To integrate a continuous function \(f(x, y)\) over a region \(R\) bounded by continuous functions \(y=g(x)\) and \(y=h(x)\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\text{,}\) use the formula
    \begin{equation*} \iint_{R} f(x, y) d A=\int_{a}^{b} \int_{g(x)}^{h(x)} f(x, y) d y d x \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.4.6.

    To find the bounds \(y=g(x)\) and \(y=h(x)\) when integrating in the order \(d y d x\) :
    🔗
    1. Draw a sketch of the region \(R\) in the \(x y\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Draw vertical lines from bottom to top.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Whichever boundary the vertical lines (from step 2) intersect first (as they enter the region) is the lower bound \(y=g(x)\) of the region.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Whichever boundary the vertical lines (from step 2) intersect second (as they leave the region) is the upper bound \(y=h(x)\) of the region.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.7.

    Evaluate \(\iint_{R} 2 x y d A\) where \(R\) is the region bounded by the parabolas \(y=\sqrt{x}\) and \(y=x^{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The surface \(f(x, y)= xy\) in blue and the net volume in orange
    🔗
    Solution.
    First we find where the two curves intersect to determine the \(x\)-bounds. Setting \(\sqrt{x} = x^2\) gives \(x = x^4\text{,}\) hence \(x(x^3-1)=0\text{,}\) so \(x=0\) or \(x=1\text{.}\) The curves meet at \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\text{,}\) giving \(0\leq x\leq 1\text{.}\) For a fixed \(x\in[0,1]\) a vertical line enters the region at \(y=x^2\) (the lower boundary, since \(x^2\leq\sqrt{x}\) on \([0,1]\)) and exits at \(y=\sqrt{x}\) (the upper boundary). Hence
    \begin{equation*} \iint_{R} 2xy\, dA = \int_{0}^{1} \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} 2xy\, dy\, dx\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Evaluating the inner integral with \(x\) treated as a constant:
    \begin{equation*} \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} 2xy\, dy = \left. xy^2 \right|_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} = x(\sqrt{x})^2 - x(x^2)^2 = x^2 - x^5\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Then integrating with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{1} (x^2 - x^5)\, dx = \left. \frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^6}{6} \right|_{0}^{1} = \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{6}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.8.

    Find the (net) volume of the prism whose base is the triangle in the \(x y\)-plane bounded by \(x\)-axis and the lines \(y=x\) and \(x=1\) and whose top lies on the plane \(z=6-3 x^2-2y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The surface \(f(x, y)=6-3 x^2-2y\) in blue and the net volume in orange
    🔗
    Solution.
    The three boundary lines \(y=0\text{,}\) \(y=x\text{,}\) and \(x=1\) meet at \((0,0)\text{,}\) \((1,0)\text{,}\) and \((1,1)\text{.}\) For a fixed \(x\in[0,1]\) a vertical line enters the triangle at \(y=0\) and exits at \(y=x\text{,}\) giving the bounds \(0\leq y\leq x\) and \(0\leq x\leq 1\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_{0}^{1} \int_{0}^{x} (6 - 3x^2 - 2y)\, dy\, dx\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Evaluating the inner integral:
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{x} (6 - 3x^2 - 2y)\, dy = \left. (6 - 3x^2)y - y^2 \right|_{0}^{x} = (6 - 3x^2)x - x^2 = 6x - 3x^3 - x^2\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Then integrating with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{1} (6x - 3x^3 - x^2)\, dx = \left. 3x^2 - \frac{3x^4}{4} - \frac{x^3}{3} \right|_{0}^{1} = 3 - \frac{3}{4} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{36}{12} - \frac{9}{12} - \frac{4}{12} = \frac{23}{12}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.4.9.

    Sometimes the region of integration \(R\) is more easily described as being bounded to the left and right instead of below and above. In this case, the boundaries are functions of \(\mathbf{y}\text{,}\) as shown below:
    🔗
    🔗
    In this case, we use cross sections parallel to the \(x\)-axis (treat \(y\) as a constant). Now, we have that the net area of the cross sections is given by
    \begin{equation*} A(y)=\int_{g(y)}^{h(y)} f(x, y) d x \end{equation*}
    and the net volume is given by
    \begin{equation*} V=\int_{c}^{d} A(y) d y \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Remark 4.4.10. Evaluating Double Integrals as Iterated Integrals.

    To integrate a continuous function \(f(x, y)\) over a region \(R\) bounded by continuous functions \(x=g(y)\) and \(x=h(y)\) from \(y=c\) to \(y=d\text{,}\) use the formula
    \begin{equation*} \iint_{R} f(x, y) d A=\int_{c}^{d} \int_{g(y)}^{h(y)} f(x, y) d x d y \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.4.11.

    To find the bounds \(x=g(y)\) and \(x=h(y)\) when integrating in the order \(d x d y\) :
    🔗
    1. Draw a sketch of the region \(R\) in the \(x y\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Draw horizontal lines from left to right.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Whichever boundary the horizontal lines intersect first (as they enter the region) is the lower (leftmost) bound \(x=g(y)\) of the region.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Whichever boundary the horizontal lines intersects second (as they leave the region) is the upper (rightmost) bound \(x=h(y)\) of the region.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.12.

    Find the (net) volume of the prism whose base is the triangle in the \(x y\)-plane bounded by \(x\)-axis and the lines \(y=x\) and \(x=1\) and whose top lies on the plane \(z=6-3 x^2-2y\) (in the order \(d x d y\) ).
    🔗
    Solution.
    The triangle has vertices \((0,0)\text{,}\) \((1,0)\text{,}\) and \((1,1)\text{.}\) For a fixed \(y\in[0,1]\) a horizontal line enters the triangle at \(x=y\) (from the line \(y=x\) solved for \(x\)) and exits at \(x=1\text{,}\) giving the bounds \(y\leq x\leq 1\) and \(0\leq y\leq 1\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_{0}^{1} \int_{y}^{1} (6 - 3x^2 - 2y)\, dx\, dy\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Evaluating the inner integral:
    \begin{gather*} \int_{y}^{1} (6 - 3x^2 - 2y)\, dx = \left. 6x - x^3 - 2yx \right|_{y}^{1} = (6 - 1 - 2y) - (6y - y^3 - 2y^2)\\ = y^3 + 2y^2 - 8y + 5\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Then integrating with respect to \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{1} (y^3 + 2y^2 - 8y + 5)\, dy = \left. \frac{y^4}{4} + \frac{2y^3}{3} - 4y^2 + 5y \right|_{0}^{1} = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{2}{3} - 4 + 5 = \frac{3}{12} + \frac{8}{12} + \frac{12}{12} = \frac{23}{12}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which matches the result from Example 4.4.8, as expected.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.4.13. Partitioning Regions of Integration.

    Suppose we are evaluating an iterated integral using the order \(d y d x\) (cross sections parallel to the \(y\)-axis). If the lower and upper bounds of \(R\) change throughout the region, you must partition \(R\) every time such a change occurs and integrate over each piece separately,
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.14.

    Using the order \(d y d x\text{,}\) set up (but do not evaluate) an iterated integral(s) that give the (net) volume of the wedgelike solid that lies beneath the surface \(z=1-x-y\) and above the region \(R\) bounded by the curve \(y= \frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}\text{,}\) the line \(y= x-1\text{,}\) and the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    The region \(R\) and its partition into \(R_{1}\) and \(R_{2}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We first find the intersection points of the three boundary curves. Setting \(\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}=x-1\) and letting \(u=\sqrt{x}\) gives \(\frac{3}{2}u=u^2-1\text{,}\) i.e. \(2u^2-3u-2=0\text{,}\) which factors as \((2u+1)(u-2)=0\text{.}\) Since \(u\geq 0\) we get \(u=2\text{,}\) so \(x=4\) and \(y=3\text{.}\) The curve meets the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\)) at \((0,0)\) and the line meets the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\)) at \((1,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For a fixed \(x\text{,}\) a vertical line crosses the region differently depending on \(x\text{:}\) for \(0\leq x\leq 1\) the lower boundary is the line \(y=0\) (\(x\)-axis) and the upper boundary is the parabola \(y=\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}\text{,}\) while for \(1\leq x\leq 4\) the lower boundary is the line \(y=x-1\) and the upper boundary remains \(y=\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^1 \int_{0}^{\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}} 1- x - y\, dy\, dx + \int_1^{4} \int_{x-1}^{\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}} 1-x-y\, dx\, dy\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.4.15.

    Similarly, when integrating in the order \(d x d y\text{,}\) if the leftmost or rightmost bounds change, you need to partition the region \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.16.

    Using the order \(d x d y\text{,}\) set up (but do not evaluate) an iterated integral(s) that gives the net volume bounded by \(f(x, y)=2\) and the \(x y\)-plane restricted to the region \(R\) bounded by \(x=0, y=2^{x}\text{,}\) and \(y=4-2 x\text{.}\) You can assume the two curves intersect only at the point (1, 2)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The exponential \(y=2^x\) passes through \((0,1)\) and the line \(y=4-2x\) has \(y\)-intercept \((0,4)\) and meets the exponential at \((1,2)\text{,}\) as mentioned above. The \(y\)-axis (\(x=0\)) meets the exponential at \((0,1)\) and the line at \((0,4)\text{.}\) For a fixed \(y\text{,}\) a horizontal line crosses the region differently depending on \(y\text{:}\) for \(1\leq y\leq 2\) the left boundary is the exponential \(x=\log_2 y\) and the right boundary is the line \(x=\frac{4-y}{2}\text{,}\) while for \(2\leq y\leq 4\) the left boundary is the \(y\)-axis \(x=0\) and the right boundary remains \(x=\frac{4-y}{2}\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_1^2 \int_{\log_2 y}^{\frac{4-y}{2}} 2\, dx\, dy + \int_2^4 \int_0^{\frac{4-y}{2}} 2\, dx\, dy\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 4.4.17.

    Sometimes, changing the order of integration simplifies the amount of work when integrating.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.4.18.

    Set up (but do not evaluate) an iterated integral(s) that gives the net volume from Example 4.4.14 Use the order \(d x d y\text{.}\) (Beneath the surface \(z=1-x-y\) and above the region \(R\) bounded by the curve \(y= \frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}\text{,}\) the line \(y= x-1\text{,}\) and the \(x\)-axis.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We first find the intersection points of the three boundary curves. Setting \(\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}=x-1\) and letting \(u=\sqrt{x}\) gives \(\frac{3}{2}u=u^2-1\text{,}\) i.e. \(2u^2-3u-2=0\text{,}\) which factors as \((2u+1)(u-2)=0\text{.}\) Since \(u\geq 0\) we do not consider the case \(u=-\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) we only get \(u=2\text{,}\) so \(x=4\) and \(y=3\text{.}\) The curve meets the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\)) at \((0,0)\) and the line meets the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\)) at \((1,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solving the boundary curves for \(x\) in terms of \(y\text{:}\) from \(y=\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}\) we get \(x=\frac{4y^2}{9}\text{,}\) and from \(y=x-1\) we get \(x=y+1\text{.}\) For a fixed \(y\in[0,3]\) a horizontal line enters the region at the curve \(x=\frac{4y^2}{9}\) and exits at the line \(x=y+1\text{,}\) with no change in which boundary is which throughout the entire interval. Hence only a single integral is needed:
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^3 \int_{\frac{4y^2}{9}}^{y+1} (1 - x - y)\, dx\, dy\text{.} \end{equation*}
