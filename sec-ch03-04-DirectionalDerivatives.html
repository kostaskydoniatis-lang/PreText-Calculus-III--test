    Section 3.4 Directional Derivatives and the Gradient

    Note 3.4.1.

    As we saw earlier, given a scalar-valued function \(z=f(x, y)\text{,}\) we can compute its rate of change in the direction of the positive \(x\)-axis (using \(f_{x}\) ) and in the direction of the positive \(y\)-axis (using \(f_{y}\) ).
    🔗
    🔗
    The next question to answer is, how do we find the rate of change of \(f(x, y)\) in any direction?
    🔗

    Remark 3.4.2.

    When we say direction, we mean a direction in the \(x y\)-plane.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.3.

    Suppose we are standing on the surface \(f(x, y)=\sin\left(y+x\right)\cos\left(y-x\right)\text{,}\) at the point \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{1}{2}\right)\) , and want to find the rate of change of \(f\) in the direction of the vector \(\vec{u}=\left\langle\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right\rangle\) (North-East).

    Note 3.4.4.

    Since we can now find rates of change in any direction, we have to modify our definition of a partial derivative to allow for change in both the \(x\) and \(y\) variables. We call this new derivative a directional derivative.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.5. Directional Derivative (Geometric).

    Given a function \(z=f(x, y)\text{,}\) an input ( \(a, b\) ), and a direction given by a nonzero unit vector \(\vec{u}=\left\langle u_{1}, u_{2}\right\rangle\text{,}\) the rate of change of \(f(x, y)\) at the input \((a, b)\) (or the point \((a, b, f(a, b)\) ) in the direction of \(\vec{u}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)=\lim _{s \rightarrow 0} \frac{f\left(a+s u_{1}, b+s u_{2}\right)-f(a, b)}{s}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We call this quantity the directional derivative of \(f\) at the input ( \(a, b\) ) in the direction of \(\vec{u}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.4.6.

    When working with directional derivatives, make sure the direction vector is a unit vector.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.7.

    Find the rate of change of \(f=x^2+y\text{,}\) at the point \((1,1,2)\text{,}\) in the direction of \(\vec{u}=\left\langle\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Here \((a,b)=(1,1)\) and \(\vec{u}=\left\langle\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right\rangle\text{,}\) which is a unit vector since \(\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{3}{4}}=1\text{.}\) Using the geometric definition of the directional derivative:
    \begin{equation*} D_{\vec{u}} f(1,1)=\lim _{s \rightarrow 0} \frac{f\left(1+\frac{1}{2}s,\ 1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}s\right)-f(1,1)}{s} \end{equation*}
    Since \(f(x,y)=x^2+y\) we have \(f(1,1)=1^2+1=2\) and
    \begin{gather*} f\left(1+\frac{1}{2}s,\ 1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}s\right)=\left(1+\frac{1}{2}s\right)^2+1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}s = 1+s+\frac{s^2}{4}+1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}s\\ = 2+\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)s+\frac{s^2}{4} \end{gather*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} D_{\vec{u}} f(1,1)=\lim _{s \rightarrow 0} \frac{2+\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)s+\frac{s^2}{4}-2}{s} = \lim _{s \rightarrow 0} \left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\frac{s}{4}\right) = 1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{equation*}
    So the rate of change of \(f\) at the point \((1,1,2)\) in the direction of \(\vec{u}\) is \(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}=\frac{2+\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.8. Directional Derivative (Computational).

    Given a differentiable function \(z=f(x, y)\text{,}\) an input \((a, b)\text{,}\) and a direction given by a nonzero unit vector \(\vec{u}=\left\langle u_{1}, u_{2}\right\rangle\text{,}\) the rate of change of \(f(x, y)\) at the input \((a, b)\) (or the point \((a, b, f(a, b)\) ) in the direction of \(\vec{u}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)=\nabla f(a, b) \cdot \vec{u} \end{equation*}
    where \(\nabla f=\left\langle f_{x}, f_{y}\right\rangle\) is called the gradient of \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.9.

    Find the directional derivative of \(f(x, y)=4 x-2 x y+y^{3}\) at the input ( \(-1,-5\) ) in the direction of the vector \(\langle 1,1\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The direction vector \(\langle 1,1\rangle\) is not a unit vector, since its length is \(\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt{2}\text{.}\) Dividing by its length gives the unit vector
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\langle 1,1\rangle = \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    The partial derivatives of \(f(x, y)=4 x-2 x y+y^{3}\) are \(f_x = 4-2y\) and \(f_y = -2x+3y^2\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f(-1,-5) = \left\langle 4-2(-5),\ -2(-1)+3(-5)^2\right\rangle = \langle 14, 77\rangle \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} D_{\vec{u}} f(-1,-5)=\nabla f(-1,-5) \cdot \vec{u} = \langle 14, 77\rangle \cdot \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right\rangle = 14\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}+77\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \frac{91\sqrt{2}}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.4.10. Motivation.

    Suppose we are standing on the point \((a, b, f(a, b))\) on a differentiable function \(f\text{.}\) What direction vector \(\vec{u}\) will maximize \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)\) ?
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.4.11.

    Given \(z=f(x, y)\text{,}\) an input \((a, b)\text{,}\) and a (nonzero) unit vector \(\vec{u}\text{;}\) if \(\nabla f(a, b) \neq 0\text{,}\) we may write
    \begin{align*} \nabla f(a, b) \cdot \vec{u} \amp =|\nabla f(a, b)||\vec{u}| \cos (\theta)\\ \amp =|\nabla f(a, b)| \cos (\theta)\text{,} \end{align*}
    where \(0 \leq \theta \leq \pi\) is the angle between \(\nabla f(a, b)\) and \(\vec{u}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.12.

    Use the work from the above remark to determine what angle \(\theta\) between \(\nabla f(a, b)\) and \(\vec{u}\) will:
    🔗
    (a) Maximize \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Minimize \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) Make \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the remark, \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)=|\nabla f(a, b)| \cos (\theta)\text{,}\) where \(|\nabla f(a, b)|\) is a fixed positive number that does not depend on \(\vec{u}\text{.}\) So the value of the directional derivative is controlled entirely by \(\cos(\theta)\text{,}\) with \(0 \leq \theta \leq \pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)\) is maximized when \(\cos(\theta)\) is as large as possible, that is \(\cos(\theta)=1 \Rightarrow \theta = 0\text{.}\) The maximum value is \(|\nabla f(a, b)|\text{,}\) attained when \(\vec{u}\) points in the same direction as \(\nabla f(a, b)\text{.}\) The unit vector in that the direction is \(\vec{u} = \frac{\nabla f(a, b)}{|\nabla f(a, b)|}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)\) is minimized when \(\cos(\theta)\) is as small as possible, that is \(\cos(\theta)=-1 \Rightarrow \theta = \pi\text{.}\) The minimum value is \(-|\nabla f(a, b)|\text{,}\) attained when \(\vec{u}\) points in the direction opposite of \(\nabla f(a, b)\text{.}\) The unit vector in that the direction is \(\vec{u} = -\frac{\nabla f(a, b)}{|\nabla f(a, b)|}\)
    🔗
    (c) \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)=0\) when \(\cos(\theta)=0 \Rightarrow \theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) that is when \(\vec{u}\) is orthogonal to \(\nabla f(a, b)\text{.}\) To find a unit vector(s) perpendicular to \(\nabla f(a, b)\) one needs to solve the system of equations \(f_x(a,b)u_x+f_y(a,b)u_y = 0\) and \(u_x^2+u_y^2=1\text{.}\) This will result in two vectors \(\vec{u}_2 = \langle u_x,u_y \rangle\) and \(\vec{u}_1 = \langle -u_x,-u_y \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.13. Steepest Ascent and Descent.

    Given a differentiable function \(z=f(x, y)\) and an input \((a, b)\) :
    🔗
    • To maximize \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)\) (move in the direction of steepest ascent), \(\vec{u}\) must be a unit vector in the direction of \(\nabla f(a, b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • To minimize \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)\) (move in the direction of steepest descent), \(\vec{u}\) must be a unit vector in the direction opposite of \(\nabla f(a, b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • To make \(D_{\vec{u}} f(a, b)=0\) (move in a direction of no ascent nor descent), \(\vec{u}\) must be a unit vector in a direction orthogonal to \(\nabla f(a, b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.4.14.

    In the definition above, when we say move, we refer to taking an infinitesimally small step in a given direction.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.15.

    Suppose we are standing on a mountain given by the surface \(z=\sqrt{9-x^{2}-y^{2}}\text{.}\) We are currently standing on the point \((2,1, z(2,1))=\left(2,1, 2\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) In what direction should we move in order to move in the direction of steepest ascent? Give your answer as a unit vector.
    🔗
    Graph of \(z=\sqrt{9-x^{2}-y^{2}}\) and \(\left(2,1, 2\right)\)
    🔗
    (b) In what direction should we move in order to minimize the rate of change of \(f\) ?
    🔗
    (c) In what direction(s) should we move in order to move in a direction of no ascent nor descent?
    🔗
    (d) If we take a unit step in the direction found in part (a), what point would we end up on on the surface \(z\) ?
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The partial derivatives of \(z=\sqrt{9-x^{2}-y^{2}}\) are
    \begin{equation*} z_x = \frac{-x}{\sqrt{9-x^{2}-y^{2}}}, \quad z_y = \frac{-y}{\sqrt{9-x^{2}-y^{2}}} \end{equation*}
    At the input \((2,1)\) we have \(\sqrt{9-2^2-1^2}=\sqrt{4}=2\text{,}\) hence
    \begin{equation*} \nabla z(2,1) = \left\langle -\frac{2}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle = \left\langle -1, -\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    (a) To move in the direction of steepest ascent we must move in the direction of \(\nabla z(2,1)\text{.}\) Since \(|\nabla z(2,1)| = \sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}\text{,}\) the unit vector is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\left\langle -1, -\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle = \left\langle -\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}, -\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\right\rangle = \left\langle -\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5}, -\frac{\sqrt{5}}{5}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    (Standing at \((2,1,2)\text{,}\) this direction points from the input \((2,1)\) towards the origin of the \(xy\)-plane, above which the top of the mountain sits.)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    (b) To minimize the rate of change we must move in the direction opposite of \(\nabla z(2,1)\text{,}\) that is \(\left\langle \frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5}, \frac{\sqrt{5}}{5}\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) We must move orthogonally to \(\nabla z(2,1)\text{:}\) a unit vector \(\left\langle u_1,u_2\right\rangle\) satisfies \(\left\langle u_1,u_2\right\rangle \cdot \left\langle -1, -\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle = 0 \Rightarrow u_2 = -2u_1\text{,}\) and then \(u_1^2+4u_1^2=1 \Rightarrow u_1 = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\text{.}\) This gives the two directions \(\left\langle \frac{\sqrt{5}}{5}, -\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5}\right\rangle\) and \(\left\langle -\frac{\sqrt{5}}{5}, \frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5}\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (d) Taking a unit step from the input \((2,1)\) in the direction found in part (a) lands on the input
    \begin{equation*} \left(2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}},\ 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\right) = \left(2-\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5},\ 1-\frac{\sqrt{5}}{5}\right) \end{equation*}
    For the corresponding point on the surface we need the value of \(z\) at this input. Since
    \begin{equation*} \left(2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\right)^2+\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\right)^2 = 4-\frac{8}{\sqrt{5}}+\frac{4}{5}+1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}+\frac{1}{5} = 6-\frac{10}{\sqrt{5}} = 6-2\sqrt{5} \end{equation*}
    we get \(z = \sqrt{9-\left(6-2\sqrt{5}\right)} = \sqrt{3+2\sqrt{5}}\text{,}\) so we end up on the point
    \begin{equation*} \left(2-\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5},\ 1-\frac{\sqrt{5}}{5},\ \sqrt{3+2\sqrt{5}}\right) \approx (1.11,\ 0.55,\ 2.73) \end{equation*}
    which is indeed higher than our starting height \(z=2\text{,}\) as expected when moving in the direction of steepest ascent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.16.

    Given the surface \(g(x, y)=\cos (y-5x)\) :
    🔗
    (a) Compute the directional derivative of \(g\) at the input \((\frac{\pi}{2},2\pi)\) in the direction of the vector \(\vec{u}=\left\langle\frac{5}{13}, \frac{12}{13}\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Does the vector \(\vec{u}\) give a direction of ascent or descent?
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The vector \(\vec{u}=\left\langle\frac{5}{13}, \frac{12}{13}\right\rangle\) is a unit vector, since \(\sqrt{\frac{25}{169}+\frac{144}{169}}=\sqrt{\frac{169}{169}}=1\text{.}\) By the chain rule, the partial derivatives of \(g(x, y)=\cos (y-5x)\) are
    \begin{equation*} g_x = -\sin(y-5x)\cdot(-5) = 5\sin(y-5x), \quad g_y = -\sin(y-5x) \end{equation*}
    At the input \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2},2\pi\right)\) we have \(y-5x = 2\pi-\frac{5\pi}{2} = -\frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=-1\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} \nabla g\left(\frac{\pi}{2},2\pi\right) = \left\langle 5(-1), -(-1)\right\rangle = \langle -5, 1\rangle \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} D_{\vec{u}} g\left(\frac{\pi}{2},2\pi\right) = \langle -5, 1\rangle \cdot \left\langle\frac{5}{13}, \frac{12}{13}\right\rangle = -\frac{25}{13}+\frac{12}{13} = -\frac{13}{13} = -1 \end{equation*}
    (b) Since \(D_{\vec{u}} g\left(\frac{\pi}{2},2\pi\right) = -1 \lt 0\text{,}\) the function decreases as we move in the direction of \(\vec{u}\text{,}\) so \(\vec{u}\) gives a direction of descent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.4.17. Directional Derivatives in Higher Dimensions.

    Everything we have said about directional derivatives in 3-dimensions extends to higher dimensions. Moreover, the gradient vector is defined similarly.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.18.

    Consider the (4-dimensional) function \(f(x, y, z)=x^{3}- y^{3}-z^3\text{.}\) In what direction is the rate of change of this surface at the input \((1,1,0)\) maximized?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Just as in 3 dimensions, the rate of change is maximized when we move in the direction of the gradient vector. Since \(f(x, y, z)=x^{3}- y^{3}-z^3\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f = \left\langle 3x^2, -3y^2, -3z^2\right\rangle \quad \text{and} \quad \nabla f(1,1,0) = \langle 3, -3, 0\rangle \end{equation*}
    Hence the rate of change at the input \((1,1,0)\) is maximized in the direction of \(\langle 3, -3, 0\rangle\text{.}\) As a unit vector, since \(|\langle 3, -3, 0\rangle| = \sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt{2}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}=\frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}\langle 3, -3, 0\rangle = \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0\right\rangle \end{equation*}
