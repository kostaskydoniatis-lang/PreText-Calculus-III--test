    Section 5.1 Vector Fields and Line Integrals

    Note 5.1.1.

    So far, we have studied two types of functions:
    🔗
    1. Vector-valued functions (one input variable, multiple output variables) of the form
      \begin{equation*} \vec{r}(t)=\langle f(t), g(t), h(t)\rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Scalar-valued functions (multiple input variables, one output variable) of the form
      \begin{equation*} f(x, y)=z\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Now, we will look at functions of multiple input variables and multiple output variables.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 5.1.2. Vector Fields in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\).

    A function of the form \(\vec{F}(x, y)=\langle f(x, y), g(x, y)\rangle\) is a vector field in the \(x y\)-plane.
    🔗
    The vector field \(\vec{F}\) assigns to each point ( \(x, y\) ) in the \(x y\)-plane, a vector \(\vec{F}(x, y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.3.

    Definition 5.1.6. Vector Fields in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\).

    A function of the form \(\mathrm{F}(x, y, z)=\langle f(x, y, z), g(x, y, z), h(x, y, z)\rangle\) is a vector field in \(x y z\)-space.
    🔗
    The vector field \(\vec{F}\) assigns to each point ( \(x, y, z\) ) in \(x y z\)-space, a vector \(\vec{F}(x, y, z)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 5.1.7.

    Suppose that an electron is moving in a magnetic field. If it is moving in the direction of the field, then it accelerates (energy is being transferred to the electron). How can we measure that energy transfer?
    🔗
    We will start by deriving a way to integrate a vector field \(\vec{F}=\langle f(x, y), g(x, y)\rangle\) over a 2D curve \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle x(t), y(t)\rangle\) in its domain. We will call such an integral a vector field line integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.8. Derivation of a Vector Field Line Integral.

    Suppose you start with an oriented curve \(C\) parametrized as \(\vec{r}(t)= \langle x(t), y(t)\rangle, a \leq t \leq b\text{,}\) and with a vector field \(\vec{F}=\langle f(x, y), g(x, y)\rangle\text{.}\) We follow a similar process as with scalar line integrals.
    🔗
    We start by partitioning \(C\) into \(n\) pieces of arc length \(\Delta s_{k}\text{.}\) Second, we pick a point ( \(x_{k}, y_{k}\) ) in the \(k\)-th segment of \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now, when computing scalar line integrals, if we were integrating a surface \(h(x, y)\text{,}\) we would multiply \(\Delta s_{k}\) by \(h\left(x_{k}, y_{k}\right)\text{.}\) When integrating a vector field, we can’t do this since \(\vec{F}\left(x_{k}, y_{k}\right)\) is a vector, not a number. Hence, we instead use the quantity \(\vec{F}_{k} \cdot \vec{T}_{k}\text{,}\) where \(\vec{F}_{k}=\vec{F}\left(x_{k}, y_{k}\right)\) and \(\vec{T}_{k}\) is the unit tangent vector to \(C \left(\frac{\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)}{\left|\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)\right|}\right)\) at the point \(\left(x_{k}, y_{k}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Finally, we add these quantities up and take the limit as the number of segments \(n\) approaches infinity to get
    \begin{equation*} \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \vec{F}_{k} \cdot \vec{T}_{k} \Delta s_{k}=\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Important: We can interpret \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s\) as adding up the scalar component of \(\vec{F}\) (the vector field) in the direction of \(\vec{T}\) (the curve) at each point along \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 5.1.9. Evaluating a Vector Field Line Integral.

    We can find a nice computational formula for \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s\) by noting that
    \begin{equation*} \vec{T}=\frac{\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)}{\left|\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)\right|}, \quad ds = \left|\vec{r}' (t) \right|dt \end{equation*}
    and hence
    \begin{equation*} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s=\left(\vec{F} \cdot \frac{\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)}{\left|\vec{r}^{\prime}(t)\right|}\right) \left|\vec{r}' (t) \right|dt=\vec{F} \cdot \vec r'(t) d t \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.10. Evaluating Vector Field Line Integral.

    Let \(C\) be a smooth curve parametrized by \(\vec{r}(t)\text{,}\) \(a \leq t \leq b\) and \(\vec{F}\) be a (continuous) vector field over a region containing \(C\text{.}\) Then the vector field line integral of \(\vec{F}\) over \(C\) is evaluated as
    \begin{equation*} \int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s=\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r}=\int_{a}^{b} \vec{F}(\vec{r}(t)) \cdot \vec{r}^{\prime}(t) d t \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 5.1.13.

    Two things to note from Example 5.1.11 and Example 5.1.12 that are true in general:
    🔗
    1. Given a curve \(C\) in the domain of a vector field \(\vec{F}\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} \int_{-C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s=-\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Given a vector field \(\vec{F}\text{,}\) two points \(P \neq Q\) in its domain, and two different curves \(C_{1}\) and \(C_{2}\) connecting \(P\) and \(Q\text{,}\) in most cases,
      \begin{equation*} \int_{C_{1}} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \neq \int_{C_{2}} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \end{equation*}
      🔗
      • In general, different curves connecting two points give different values for line integrals.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.14. Direction Interpretation.

    Given a vector field \(\vec{F}\text{,}\) a curve \(C\) parametrized as \(\vec{r}(t)\text{,}\) and a point ( \(x_{k}, y_{k}\) ) on the curve, since \(\vec{F}_{k} \cdot \vec{T}_{k}\) can be positive or negative (depending on the angle between the vectors), the line integral can be positive or negative.
    🔗
    Hence, we can sometimes determine whether \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r}\) is positive or negative by looking at the angles between the vectors:
    🔗
    • If for most of the curve, the angle \(\theta\) between \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{T}\) satisfies \(0 \leq \theta \lt \frac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) then we expect \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r} \gt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If for most of the curve, the angle \(\theta\) between \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{T}\) satisfies \(\frac{\pi}{2} \lt \theta \leq \pi\text{,}\) then we expect \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r} \lt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • It is also possible for \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r}=0\) (for example, if \(\vec{F} \cdot \vec{T}=0\) for all points on \(C)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.16.

    When a force (given by the vector field vectors) acts on an object/particle moving along a curve (a particle/curve system), we say the vector field is doing work on the system.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 5.1.17. Vector Field Line Integrals as Work.

    Let \(\vec{F}\) be a continuous force field and let \(C\) be a smooth oriented curve in its domain. The work done by \(\vec{F}\) on the system given by a particle moving along \(C\) is
    \begin{equation*} W=\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.18.

    Find the work done by the force field \(\vec{F}=\left\langle x,z,-y\right\rangle\) acting on the curve/particle system given by \(\vec{r}(t)=\left\langle t^2, t^{3}, t\right\rangle, 0 \leq t \leq 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{gather*} \vec r(t)=\left\langle t^2, t^{3}, t\right\rangle \Rightarrow x(t) = t^2, \ y(t) = t^3, \ z(t) = t,\ 0\leq t \leq 1 \Rightarrow \vec F (\vec r(t)) = \langle t^2 , t , -t^3 \rangle\\ \vec r' (t) = \langle 2t , 3t^2 , 1 \rangle, \Rightarrow \vec F (\vec r(t))\cdot \vec r' (t) = t^2\cdot 2t + t\cdot 3t^2 +-t^3 \cdot 1 = 4t^3\\ \mathbf W = \int_C \vec F \cdot \vec T \ ds = \int_0^1 \vec F (\vec r(t))\cdot \vec r' (t) \ dt =\int_0^1 4t^3\ dt = \left . t^4 \right |_0^1 =1 \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.19. Work Interpretation.

    Under the work interpretation, it is important to understand what positive, negative, and zero work mean:
    🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r} \gt 0\text{,}\) energy is being gained by the curve/particle system (in the form of kinetic and potential energy).
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r} \lt 0\text{,}\) energy is being lost by the curve/particle system (in the form of heat, friction, etc.).
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r}=0\text{,}\) this does not mean the vector field forces didn’t affect the system, more generally, the amount of energy gained was the same as the energy lost.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 5.1.20.

    Recall: A curve \(C\) is:
    🔗
    • Smooth: If it has no sharp edges/corners.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Closed: If it is a loop (has no discernible starting/ending point).
      🔗
      🔗
    • Simple: If it doesn’t cross itself.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    We call a curve \(C\) that is simple, closed, and smooth, a SCS curve.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.21.

    Suppose you have a 2-dimensional vector field \(\vec{F}\) and a SCS curve \(C\) in its domain. We define the circulation of \(\vec{F}\) along \(C\) as a measure of how much the vector field (vectors) points in the direction of \(C\) (the unit tangent vectors of \(C)\text{.}\) That is, we define the circulation of \(\vec{F}\) along \(C\) precisely as
    \begin{equation*} \int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 5.1.22. Vector Field Line Integrals as Circulation.

    Let \(\vec{F}\) be a 2-dimensional continuous force field and let \(C\) be a SCS curve in its domain. The circulation of \(\vec{F}\) along \(C\) is computed as
    \begin{equation*} \int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.23. Circulation Interpretation.

    If the path \(C\) is not closed that we call this Flow. When we are talking about circulation along a boundary, the sign of the integral tells us the following:
    🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \gt 0\text{,}\) most of the vector field is in the direction of \(C\text{.}\) Hence, most particles (whose motion is represented by \(\vec{F}\) ) are moving along \(C\) in the same orientation (as \(C\) ).
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \lt 0\text{,}\) most of the vector field is in the direction opposite of \(C\text{.}\) Hence, most particles are moving along \(C\) in the opposite orientation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s=0\text{,}\) there is no discernible motion along \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.24.

    Given the vector field \(\vec{F}=\langle x-y, -y\rangle\) and the curve \(C\) parametrized by \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle\sin (t), \cos (t)\rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \leq t \leq 2 \pi\text{,}\) determine whether most of the vector field is in the direction of \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{gather*} \vec r(t)=\langle\sin (t), \cos (t)\rangle \Rightarrow \vec F (\vec r(t)) = \langle \sin (t) - \cos (t), - \cos (t) \rangle, \quad \vec r' (t) = \langle \cos (t) , -\sin (t)\rangle\\ \vec F (\vec r(t))\cdot \vec r' (t) = \sin (t)\cos (t) -\cos^2 (t) + \cos(t)\sin (t) = -\cos^2 (t) + 2\sin (t) \cos (t)\\ \int_C \vec F \cdot \vec T \ ds = \int_0^{2\pi} \vec F (\vec r(t))\cdot \vec r' (t) \ dt =\int_0^{2\pi} - \cos^2 (t) +2\sin (t)\cos (t)\ dt =\\ -\int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2 (t) \ dt +2\int_0^{2\pi} +\sin (t)\cos (t)\ dt \underset{\substack{t= 0 \Rightarrow u = 0\\ t =2\pi \Rightarrow u =0}}{\overset{\substack{u=\sin t \\ du = \cos t dt}}{=}} -\int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2 (t)\ dt +2\int_0^0 u\ du =\\ -\int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2 (t) dt +0 \lt 0.\\ \text{Hence most of the vector field is in the opposite direction of}C \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 5.1.25.

    Although we will mostly work with SCS curves when talking about circulation, we can generalize this idea to curves that are piece-wise smooth (a union of smooth curves connected at their endpoints).
    🔗
    We call a simple, closed, piece-wise smooth curve \(C\) a SCPS curve.
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{gather*} C: \vec r (t) = \begin{cases} \text{Line segment of the y axis}\amp\quad 0\leq t \leq 1\\ \text{Line segment of the x axis}\amp\quad 1\leq t \leq 2\\ \text{Unit circle, first quadrant } \amp\quad 2\leq t \leq 2+\frac{\pi}{2}\\ \end{cases} =\\ \begin{cases} \langle 0,1 \rangle + t \langle 0 , -1 \rangle = \langle 0,1-t \rangle\amp\quad 0\leq t \leq 1\\ \langle 0,0 \rangle + (t-1) \langle 1 , 0 \rangle = \langle 1-t, 0 \rangle\amp\quad 1\leq t \leq 2\\ \langle \cos (t-2), \sin (t-2) \rangle \amp\quad 2\leq t \leq 2+\frac{\pi}{2}\\ \end{cases}\\ \vec r' (t) = \begin{cases} \langle 0,-1 \rangle\amp\quad 0\lt t \lt 1\\ \langle 1, 0 \rangle\amp\quad 1\lt t \lt 2\\ \langle -\sin (t-2), \cos (t-2) \rangle \amp\quad 2 \lt t \lt 2+\frac{\pi}{2}\\ \end{cases}\\ \vec F ( \vec r (t)) = \begin{cases} \langle 1 , 0\rangle\amp\quad 0\leq t \leq 1\\ \langle 1, t-1 \rangle\amp\quad 1\leq t \leq 2\\ \langle 1, \cos (t-2) \rangle \amp\quad 2\leq t \leq 2+\frac{\pi}{2}\\ \end{cases}\\ \vec F ( \vec r (t)) \cdot \vec r'(t) = \begin{cases} 1\cdot 0 + 0\cdot (-1) = 0\amp\quad 0\lt t \lt 1\\ 1\cdot 1 + (t-1)\cdot 0 = 1\amp\quad 1\lt t \lt 2\\ -\sin (t-2) +\cos^2 (t-2) \amp\quad 2 \lt t \lt 2+\frac{\pi}{2}\\ \end{cases}\\ \int_{C} \vec F \cdot \vec T \ ds = \int_0^1 0 \ dt + \int_1^2 1 \ dt + \int_2^{2+\frac{\pi}{2}} -\sin (t-2) +\cos^2 (t-2) \ dt \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 5.1.27.

    In defining the circulation of a vector field \(\vec{F}\) along a closed curve \(C\text{,}\) we were interested in the scalar component of the vector field in the direction of \(C\) (measured using \(\vec{T}\) ), which is why we used \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.28. Flux of a Vector Field.

    For flux, instead of wanting to measure how much of the vector field is in the direction of \(C\text{,}\) we instead want to measure how much of the vector field is normal/perpendicular/orthogonal to \(C\text{.}\) Hence, instead of using the scalar component of \(\vec{F}\) in the direction of a unit tangent vector \(\vec{T}\text{,}\) we will use the scalar component of \(\vec{F}\) in the direction of \(\vec{N}\) (a unit vector normal to \(C\) ). Hence, we will define the flux of \(\vec{F}\) on across \(C\) as \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{N} d s\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 5.1.29. Vector Field Line Integrals as Flux.

    Let \(\vec{F}=\langle f(x, y), g(x, y)\rangle\) be a 2 -dimensional continuous vector field and let \(C\) be a SCPS oriented curve in its domain parametrized by \(\vec{r}(t)= \langle x(t), y(t)\rangle, a \leq t \leq b\) and oriented counter clockwise. Let \(\vec n(t) = \langle y'(t), -x'(t) \rangle\text{,}\) a normal vector that points to the right of the direction that the curve is parametrized with, and \(\vec N\) the unit normal vector in its direction. The flux of \(\vec{F}\) across \(C\) is computed as
    \begin{equation*} \int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{N} d s= \int_{a}^b \vec{F}(\vec r (t)) \cdot \vec n(t) d t=\int_{a}^{b}\left(f(x(t), y(t)) y^{\prime}(t)-g(x(t), y(t)) x^{\prime}(t)\right) d t \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.1.30.

    When we are talking about flux across a boundary, the sign of the integral tells us the following:
    🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{N} d s \gt 0\text{,}\) most of the vector field points away from \(C\text{.}\) Hence, most particles (whose motion is represented by \(\vec{F}\) ) are moving from inside \(R\) towards the outside.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{N} d s \lt 0\text{,}\) most of the vector field points towards (the inside of) \(C\text{.}\) Hence, most particles are moving from outside \(R\) towards the inside.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{N} d s=0\text{,}\) the same amount of particles (could be none) are entering and exiting the region \(R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.1.31.

    Determine whether most particles, whose motion is represented by \(\vec{F}=\langle x+y, x\rangle\text{,}\) are entering or exiting across the ellipse \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle 2 \cos (t), \sin (t)\rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \leq t \leq 2 \pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{gather*} \vec r(t)=\langle 2 \cos (t), \sin (t)\rangle \Rightarrow \vec F (\vec r(t)) = \langle 2\cos (t) +\sin (t),\cos (t) \rangle\\ \vec r' (t) = \langle -2\sin (t),\cos (t)\rangle \overset{\vec n (r) = \langle y' (t), - x'(t)\rangle}{\Rightarrow} \vec n (t) = \langle \cos (t) , 2 \sin (t) \rangle\\ \vec F (\vec r(t))\cdot \vec n (t) = (2\cos (t) +\sin (t))\cos (t) +2\cos (t) 2\sin (t) = 2\cos^2 (t) + 5\cos(t)\sin (t)\\ \int_C \vec F \cdot \vec N \ ds = \int_0^{2\pi} \vec F (\vec r(t))\cdot \vec n (t) \ dt =\int_0^{2\pi} 2 \cos^2 (t) +5\sin (t)\cos (t)\ dt =\\ 2\int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2 (t) \ dt +5\int_0^{2\pi} +\sin (t)\cos (t)\ dt \underset{\substack{t= 0 \Rightarrow u = 0\\ t =2\pi \Rightarrow u =0}}{\overset{\substack{u=\sin t \\ du = \cos t dt}}{=}} 2\int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2 (t)\ dt +5\cancelto{0}{\int_0^0 u\ du} =\\ 2\left ( \cancelto{0}{\left .\frac{1}{2}\cos (t)\sin (t) \right |_0^{2\pi}} + \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^0 (t)\ dt\right ) = 2\cdot\frac{1}{2} \int_0^{2\pi}\ dt = 2\pi \gt 0 .\\ \text{ Hence most of the particles are exiting the ellipse, since it is parametrized counter-clockwise.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    Example 5.1.32.

    Determine whether most particles, whose motion is represented by \(\vec{F}=\langle y, x\rangle\text{,}\) are entering or exiting across the ellipse \(\vec{r}(t)=\langle \cos (t), \sin (t)\rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \leq t \leq 2 \pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{gather*} \vec r(t)=\langle \cos (t), \sin (t)\rangle \Rightarrow \vec F (\vec r(t)) = \langle \sin (t),\cos (t) \rangle\\ \vec r' (t) = \langle -\sin (t),\cos (t)\rangle \overset{\vec n (r) = \langle y' (t), - x'(t)\rangle}{\Rightarrow} \vec n (t) = \langle \cos (t) , \sin (t) \rangle\\ \vec F (\vec r(t))\cdot \vec n (t) = \sin (t)\cos (t) +\cos (t) \sin (t) = 2\cos(t)\sin (t)\\ \int_C \vec F \cdot \vec N \ ds = \int_0^{2\pi} \vec F (\vec r(t))\cdot \vec n (t) \ dt =\int_0^{2\pi} 2 \sin (t)\cos (t)\ dt \underset{\substack{t= 0 \Rightarrow u = 0\\ t =2\pi \Rightarrow u =0}}{\overset{\substack{u=\sin t \\ du = \cos t dt}}{=}}\\ 2\cancelto{0}{\int_0^0 u\ du} =0\\ \text{ Hence the same amount of particles are exiting and entering the circle.} \end{gather*}

    Remark 5.1.33. Summary of Interpretations.

    For the line integral \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s\) :
    🔗
    • Directional interpretation.
      🔗
      1. If for most of the curve, the angle \(\theta\) between \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{T}\) satisfies \(0 \leq \theta \lt \frac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) then we expect \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r} \gt 0\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      2. If for most of the curve, the angle \(\theta\) between \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{T}\) satisfies \(\frac{\pi}{2} \lt \theta \leq \pi\text{,}\) then we expect \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r} \lt 0\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      3. It is also possible for \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r}=0\) (for example, if \(\vec{F} \cdot \vec{T}=0\) for all points on \(C\) ).
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • Work interpretation.
      🔗
      1. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r} \gt 0\text{,}\) energy is being gained by the curve/particle system (in the form of kinetic and potential energy).
        🔗
        🔗
      2. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r} \lt 0\text{,}\) energy is being lost by the curve/particle system (in the form of heat, friction, etc.).
        🔗
        🔗
      3. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d \vec{r}=0\text{,}\) this does not mean the vector field forces didn’t affect the system, more generally, the amount of energy gained was the same as the energy lost.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • Circulation Interpretation (if \(C\) is simple, closed, piece-wise smooth):
      🔗
      1. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \gt 0\text{,}\) most of the vector field is in the direction of \(C\text{.}\) Hence, most particles (whose motion is represented by \(\vec{F}\) ) are moving along \(C\) in the same orientation (as \(C\) ).
        🔗
        🔗
      2. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s \lt 0\text{,}\) most of the vector field is in the direction opposite of \(C\text{.}\) Hence, most particles are moving along \(C\) in the opposite orientation.
        🔗
        🔗
      3. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{T} d s=0\text{,}\) there is no discernible motion along \(C\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    For the integral \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{n} d s\text{,}\) when \(C\) is a simple, closed, piece-wise smooth curve enclosing a region \(R\) , oriented counter-clockwise:
    🔗
    • Flux interpretation :
      🔗
      1. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{N} d s \gt 0\text{,}\) most of the vector field points away from \(C\text{.}\) Hence, most particles (whose motion is represented by \(\vec{F}\) ) are moving from inside \(R\) towards the outside.
        🔗
        🔗
      2. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{N} d s \lt 0\text{,}\) most of the vector field points towards (the inside of) \(C\text{.}\) Hence, most particles are moving from outside \(R\) towards the inside.
        🔗
        🔗
      3. If \(\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot \vec{N} d s=0\text{,}\) the same amount of particles (could be none) are entering and exiting the region \(R\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
