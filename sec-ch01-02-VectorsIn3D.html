    Section 1.2 Vectors in 3D

    Note 1.2.1.

    Everything we did with vectors in the previous section transfers to vectors in 3D (almost identically). Hence, we will focus first on getting used to three dimensions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.2.2. \(xyz\)-Coordinate System.

    The 3-dimensional coordinate system represented by the \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\) axes is called the \(xyz\)-coordinate system.
    🔗
    Other names for this coordinate system are:
    🔗
    • Euclidean space
      🔗
      🔗
    • 3-dimensional space (3D space)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(xyz\)-space
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\mathbb{R}^{3}=\{(x, y, z) \mid x, y, z \in \mathbb{R}\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.2.6. Points in \(xyz\)-Space.

    In the \(xy\)-plane, we represent points by ordered pairs of the form \(\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)\text{.}\) In \(xyz\)-space, we represent points by ordered triples of the form \(\left(x_{1}, y_{1}, z_{1}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Given a point \(\left(x_{1}, y_{1}, z_{1}\right)\text{,}\) we call \(x_{1}\text{,}\) \(y_{1}\text{,}\) and \(z_{1}\) the \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\) coordinates of the point, respectively.
    🔗
    🔗
    We have infinitely many planes. The “main” planes are:
    🔗
    • The \(xy\)-plane: Consists of all points with \(z\)-coordinate equal to zero. Defined by the equation \(z=0\text{.}\) Contains the \(x\) and \(y\) axes.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The \(xz\)-plane: Consists of all points with \(y\)-coordinate equal to zero. Defined by the equation \(y=0\text{.}\) Contains the \(x\) and \(z\) axes.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The \(yz\)-plane: Consists of all points with \(x\)-coordinate equal to zero. Defined by the equation \(x=0\text{.}\) Contains the \(y\) and \(z\) axes.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.9.

    Sketch the plane parallel to the \(xz\)-plane through \((1,9,4)\text{.}\) Moreover, write an equation for this plane.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The \(xz\)-plane is defined by the equation \(y=0\text{,}\) and any plane parallel to it consists of all points whose \(y\)-coordinate is equal to one fixed constant. Since our plane must contain the point \((1,9,4)\text{,}\) that constant is 9, so the plane is defined by the equation
    \begin{equation*} y = 9 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To sketch it, draw the \(xyz\)-axes (following the right-hand rule), mark the value 9 on the \(y\)-axis, and through that mark draw a copy of the \(xz\)-plane. The plane contains the point \((1,9,4)\) and, more generally, every point of the form \((x, 9, z)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.2.10. Vectors in \(xyz\)-Space.

    In the \(xy\)-plane, we represent vectors in standard position using the notation \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle x_{1}, y_{1}\right\rangle\text{.}\) In \(xyz\)-space, we represent vectors in standard position using the notation \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle x_{1}, y_{1}, z_{1}\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If a vector is given by its starting point \(P\left(x_{1}, y_{1}, z_{1}\right)\) and ending point \(Q\left(x_{2}, y_{2}, z_{2}\right)\text{,}\) we can shift it to standard position by using the formula
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=\left\langle x_{2}-x_{1}, y_{2}-y_{1}, z_{2}-z_{1}\right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A vector in standard position is also called a position vector.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.2.12. Vector Operations in \(xyz\)-Space.

    Vector operations in \(xyz\)-space work exactly the same as in the \(xy\)-plane. We do operations coordinate-wise.
    🔗
    Let \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}, v_{3}\right\rangle\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{u}=\left\langle u_{1}, u_{2}, u_{3}\right\rangle\text{,}\) and \(c\) be a real number (scalar). Then
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}=\left\langle v_{1}+u_{1}, v_{2}+u_{2}, v_{3}+u_{3}\right\rangle\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}=\left\langle v_{1}-u_{1}, v_{2}-u_{2}, v_{3}-u_{3}\right\rangle\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(c \mathbf{v}=\left\langle c v_{1}, c v_{2}, c v_{3}\right\rangle\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Note: Geometrically, everything works the same. Adding/subtracting vectors can be done using the Triangle Rule. Scaling a vector changes its length and potentially flips the direction of the vector. It is harder to draw now, though...
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.13.

    Given \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 3,2,3\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 1,2,1\rangle\text{,}\) compute \(2|\mathbf{v}-2\mathbf{u}|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Working from the inside out, we first compute the vector \(\mathbf{v}-2\mathbf{u}\) coordinate-wise:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}-2\mathbf{u} = \langle 3,2,3 \rangle - \langle 2,4,2 \rangle = \langle 1,-2,1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Its magnitude is
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf{v}-2\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{1^{2}+(-2)^{2}+1^{2}} = \sqrt{6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and hence \(2|\mathbf{v}-2\mathbf{u}| = 2\sqrt{6}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.2.14. Distance and Midpoint Formula.

    Given two points \(P\left(x_{1}, y_{1}, z_{1}\right)\) and \(Q\left(x_{2}, y_{2}, z_{2}\right)\) in \(xyz\)-space, we denote the line segment connecting \(P\) and \(Q\) by \(PQ\text{.}\) Moreover, we have that
    🔗
    • The distance between the points \(P\) and \(Q\) (the length of the line segment \(PQ\)) is given by
      \begin{equation*} |P Q|=\sqrt{\left(x_{2}-x_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(y_{2}-y_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(z_{2}-z_{1}\right)^{2}} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • The midpoint on the line segment \(PQ\) is given by
      \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}, \frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}, \frac{z_{1}+z_{2}}{2}\right) \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Note that the distance between \(P\) and \(Q\) is simply \(|\overrightarrow{P Q}|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.2.16. Magnitude.

    Given a 3D vector \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}, v_{3}\right\rangle\text{,}\) then \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}}\text{.}\) As before, \(\mathbf{v}\) is a unit vector if \(|\mathbf{v}|=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.2.17. Recall: Unit Vector in the Direction of \(\mathbf{v}\).

    When working with a 3D vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) we can’t define its direction by a single angle anymore (since \(\mathbf{v}\) is contained in infinitely many planes). Instead, we focus on the unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Given a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}, v_{3}\right\rangle\text{,}\) the vector \(\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}\) is a unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.18.

    Given \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 1,2,1\rangle\text{,}\) find:
    🔗
    (a) A unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) All vectors of length 3 parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The magnitude of \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{1^{2}+2^{2}+1^{2}} = \sqrt{6}\text{,}\) so a unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\langle 1,2,1 \rangle = \left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}, \frac{2}{\sqrt{6}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    A vector of length 3 parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\) points either in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) or in the direction opposite of \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) so the only two such vectors are
    \begin{equation*} 3\mathbf{u} = \left\langle \frac{3}{\sqrt{6}}, \frac{6}{\sqrt{6}}, \frac{3}{\sqrt{6}} \right\rangle \quad \text{and} \quad -3\mathbf{u} = \left\langle -\frac{3}{\sqrt{6}}, -\frac{6}{\sqrt{6}}, -\frac{3}{\sqrt{6}} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.2.19. Coordinate Vectors.

    In \(xyz\)-space, the following vectors are called coordinate vectors:
    🔗
    • \(\mathbf{i}=\langle 1,0,0\rangle\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\mathbf{j}=\langle 0,1,0\rangle\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\mathbf{k}=\langle 0,0,1\rangle\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Given a vector \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}, v_{3}\right\rangle\text{,}\) we can also write \(\mathbf{v}\) in the form
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=v_{1} \mathbf{i}+v_{2} \mathbf{j}+v_{3} \mathbf{k} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.20.

    Verify that \(\langle -2,3,1\rangle=-2 \mathbf{i}+3 \mathbf{j}+ \mathbf{k}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Writing each scaled coordinate vector in component form and adding coordinate-wise:
    \begin{gather*} -2\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k} = -2 \langle 1,0,0\rangle + 3 \langle 0,1,0\rangle + \langle 0,0,1\rangle = \langle -2,0,0 \rangle + \langle 0,3,0 \rangle + \langle 0,0,1 \rangle\\ = \langle -2+0+0, 0+3+0, 0+0+1 \rangle = \langle -2,3,1 \rangle \end{gather*}
    🔗
    as claimed.
