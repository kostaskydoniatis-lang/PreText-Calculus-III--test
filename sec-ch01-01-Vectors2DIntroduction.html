    Section 1.1 Vectors in 2D

    Definition 1.1.1. Vectors.

    A vector is an object defined by
    🔗
    1. its magnitude (length)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. and its direction.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    They are geometrically represented by rays/arrows and symbolically with a lowercase letter with an arrow above it or in bold face (such as \(\vec{u}\) or \(\mathbf{u}, \vec{v}\) or \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) etc.).
    🔗
    🔗
    We will be drawing 2D vectors in the \(x y\)-plane (Cartesian plane). In this setting, we can define a vector by stating its starting point \(P\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)\) (arrow tail) and ending point \(Q\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)\) (arrow head).
    🔗
    When a vector is given by its starting point \(P\) and ending point \(Q\text{,}\) we can represent the vector symbolically using \(\overrightarrow{P Q}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.1.2.

    Draw the vectors below with given starting and ending points:
    🔗
    (a) \(P(0,0)\) and \(Q(3,2)\)
    🔗
    (b) \(R(0.5,2)\) and \(S(3.5,4)\)
    🔗
    What can we say about the vectors \(\overrightarrow{P Q}\) and \(\overrightarrow{R S}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Moving from \(P(0,0)\) to \(Q(3,2)\) we travel 3 units in the \(x\) direction and 2 units in the \(y\) direction. Moving from \(R(0.5,2)\) to \(S(3.5,4)\) we travel \(3.5-0.5 = 3\) units in the \(x\) direction and \(4-2 = 2\) units in the \(y\) direction. Since the two arrows describe the same displacement, they have the same magnitude and the same direction; the only difference between them is where they start.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.1.3. Equivalent Vectors.

    Vectors are said to be equivalent vectors if they have the same magnitude and direction (independent of their starting and ending points).
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.4. Vectors in Standard Position.

    If the starting point of a vector is the origin (the point \((0,0)\) ), we say the vector is in standard position.
    🔗
    Given a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) in standard position, if its ending point is ( \(v_{1}, v_{2}\) ), we write down the vector \(\mathbf{v}\) as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and we say that \(v_{1}\) is the \(x\)-component of \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(v_{2}\) is the \(y\)-component of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.1.5.

    [Writing a Vector in Standard Position]
    🔗
    Suppose a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) has starting point \(P\left(x_{1}, y_{1}\right)\) and ending point \(Q\left(x_{2}, y_{2}\right)\text{.}\) The ending point of \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) in standard position, is \(\left(x_{2}-x_{1}, y_{2}-y_{1}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In other words,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}=\left\langle x_{2}-x_{1}, y_{2}-y_{1}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Writing a vector in standard position simply shifts the vector so that its starting point is the origin.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.6.

    Graph the vector with starting point \(P(2,1)\) and ending point \(Q(-1,0)\text{;}\) write the vector in standard position; and then graph the vector in standard position.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both graphs are shown above. Using the formula, the ending point of the vector in standard position is \(\left(x_{2}-x_{1}, y_{2}-y_{1}\right)\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}= \langle -1-2, 0-1 \rangle = \langle -3,-1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    This is the arrow starting at the origin and ending at \((-3,-1)\text{:}\) the original vector shifted so that its starting point is the origin.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.7. Magnitude of a Vector.

    To find the magnitude/length of a vector:
    🔗
    1. Put the vector in standard position.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If the vector is given by \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}\right\rangle\text{,}\) then its magnitude, denoted by |v|, is given by
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.8.

    Find the length of the vector with starting point \(P(1,-2)\) and ending point \(Q(4,2)\text{.}\) Graph this vector (in standard position) and verify that the formula for length is the Pythagorean Theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    In standard position the vector is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}= \langle 4-1, 2-(-2) \rangle = \langle 3,4 \rangle \end{equation*}
    so its length is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{3^{2}+4^{2}}= \sqrt{9+16}= \sqrt{25}= 5 \end{equation*}
    As the graph above shows, in standard position the vector is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose horizontal leg has length 3 (the \(x\)-component) and whose vertical leg has length 4 (the \(y\)-component). The magnitude formula squares the two legs, adds them, and takes the square root, which is exactly the Pythagorean Theorem: \(3^{2}+4^{2} = 5^{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.9. Direction of a Vector.

    To find the direction of a vector:
    🔗
    1. Put the vector in standard position.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If the vector is given by \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}\right\rangle\text{,}\) its direction is the angle \(\theta\) starting on the positive x -axis and measured counter-clockwise ending on the vector \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}\right\rangle\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.10.

    Find the direction of \(\mathbf{v}=\langle-2,-4\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both components of \(\mathbf{v}= \langle -2,-4 \rangle\) are negative, so the vector lies in the third quadrant and its direction angle satisfies \(\pi < \theta < \frac{3\pi}{2}\text{.}\) The angle \(\phi\) that \(\mathbf{v}\) makes with the negative \(x\)-axis satisfies
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \tan(\phi) = \frac{4}{2}= 2 \Rightarrow \phi = \arctan(2) \end{equation*}
    Hence, measuring counter-clockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \pi+\arctan(2) \approx 4.25 \text{ radians}\approx 243.43^{\circ} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.1.11.

    The zero vector \(\mathbf{0}=\langle 0,0\rangle\) is the only vector with zero magnitude and no direction.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.1.12.

    From now on, any time a vector is mentioned, assume it is in standard position (unless stated otherwise).
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.1.13.

    [Adding and Subtracting Vectors]
    🔗
    To add/subtract two vectors \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{u}\text{,}\) you add/subtract the corresponding \(x\) and \(y\) components.
    🔗
    The result of adding/subtracting two vectors is again a vector.
    🔗
    🔗
    Important: Vector subtraction is not commutative. Hence, \(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}\neq \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\) (unless \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}\) ).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.14.

    Compute \(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}\) where \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 3,-2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 5,-1\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Adding and subtracting the corresponding components:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}= \langle 3+5, -2+(-1) \rangle = \langle 8,-3 \rangle \\ \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}= \langle 3-5, -2-(-1) \rangle = \langle -2,-1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    Note that both results are again vectors, and that computing \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}= \langle 2,1 \rangle\) instead would give a different (opposite) vector.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.1.15.

    There is a geometric interpretation for vector addition/subtraction. We can see it using the Triangle Rule.
    🔗
    The Triangle Rule states that in order to compute \(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    1. Draw \(\mathbf{v}\) in standard position.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Starting at the endpoint of \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) draw the vector \(\mathbf{u}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Draw a vector starting at \((0,0)\) and ending at the end of the vector \(\mathbf{u}\) as drawn in step 2.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.16.

    Given \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 1,1\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 1,-2\rangle\text{,}\) graph \(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\) using the Triangle rule.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Following the Triangle Rule, we first draw \(\mathbf{v}\) in standard position, ending at \((1,1)\text{.}\) Starting at that endpoint we draw \(\mathbf{u}\text{,}\) which takes us 1 unit right and 2 units down, ending at \((1+1, 1-2) = (2,-1)\text{.}\) Finally we draw the vector starting at \((0,0)\) and ending at \((2,-1)\text{,}\) which is the sum
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}= \langle 2,-1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    in agreement with adding the vectors component-wise, as the graph above shows.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In order to graph \(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}\) using the Triangle Rule, draw it as \(\mathbf{v}+(-\mathbf{u})\text{.}\) We define the meaning of \(-\mathbf{u}\) next.
    🔗

    Remark 1.1.17. Multiplying a Vector by a Scalar (Number).

    To multiply a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) by a scalar (number) \(c\text{,}\) you multiply the \(x\) and \(y\) components of \(\mathbf{v}\) by \(c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The result of multiplying a vector by a scalar is again a vector denoted by \(c \mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.18.

    Given the vector \(\mathbf{v}=\langle-1,1\rangle\text{,}\) compute:
    🔗
    (a) \(4 \mathbf{v}\)
    🔗
    (b) \(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}\)
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    Multiplying each component of \(\mathbf{v}\) by 4:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4\mathbf{v}= \langle 4(-1), 4(1) \rangle = \langle -4,4 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Multiplying each component of \(\mathbf{v}\) by \(-\frac{1}{2}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}= \left\langle -\frac{1}{2}(-1), -\frac{1}{2}(1) \right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.1.19.

    There is a geometric interpretation for multiplying a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) by a number \(c\text{:}\)
    🔗
    (a) If \(c>1\text{,}\) you stretch \(\mathbf{v}\) to a length of \(c|\mathbf{v}|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) If \(0<c<1\text{,}\) you compress \(\mathbf{v}\) to a length of \(c|\mathbf{v}|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) If \(c=-1\text{,}\) you flip the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (d) You can combine (c) with (a) and (b) to flip and stretch/compress at the same time.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.20.

    Graph the vectors in example 1.7 by first graphing \(\mathbf{v}\) and then using the above note.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We first graph \(\mathbf{v}= \langle -1,1 \rangle\text{,}\) which has length \(|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{2}\text{.}\) For \(4\mathbf{v}\text{:}\) since \(c=4>1\text{,}\) we stretch \(\mathbf{v}\) to a length of \(4|\mathbf{v}| = 4\sqrt{2}\) keeping its direction, ending at \((-4,4)\text{.}\) For \(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}\text{:}\) combining (c) with (b) in the note above, we flip the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) and compress it to a length of \(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{v}| = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\text{,}\) ending at \(\left( \frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}\right)\text{.}\) Both graphs are shown in the figures below.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.21. Unit and Parallel Vectors.

    A vector of length 1 is called a unit vector.
    🔗
    Given a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}\right\rangle\text{,}\) the vector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{v}|}\mathbf{v}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is a unit vector with the same direction as \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\)
    🔗
    Two vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are parallel if they lie on the same line.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.22.

    Find a unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 1,2\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Find all vectors of length 3 parallel to the vector \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 1,2\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The magnitude of \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{1^{2}+2^{2}}= \sqrt{5}\text{,}\) so a unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}= \frac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}= \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\langle 1,2 \rangle = \left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}, \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    Indeed, \(|\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{\frac{1}{5}+\frac{4}{5}}= \sqrt{1}= 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    A vector of length 3 parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\) must point either in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) or in the direction opposite of \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) so we scale the unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\) from the first part by \(3\) and by \(-3\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3\mathbf{u}= \left\langle \frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}, \frac{6}{\sqrt{5}}\right\rangle \quad \text{and}\quad -3\mathbf{u}= \left\langle -\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}, -\frac{6}{\sqrt{5}}\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    These are the only two such vectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.1.23. Components of a Vector.

    Given a vector \(\mathbf{v}=\left\langle v_{1}, v_{2}\right\rangle\text{,}\) we have the formulas
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v_{1}=|\mathbf{v}| \cos (\theta) \quad \text{ and }\quad v_{2}=|\mathbf{v}| \sin (\theta), \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(\theta\) is the angle giving the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.1.25.

    You can represent a force by using a vector. In this context, the length of the vector is the magnitude of the force.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.26. Openstax, Calculus Volume 3, Section 2.1, Question 49.

    Two forces, a horizontal force of 45 lb and another of 52 lb, act on the same object. The angle between these forces is \(25^{\circ}\text{.}\) Find the magnitude and direction angle from the positive x-axis of the resultant force that acts on the object. (Round to two decimal places.)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We place the horizontal 45 lb force along the positive \(x\)-axis, so that \(\mathbf{F}_{1} = \langle 45,0 \rangle\text{.}\) The 52 lb force then makes an angle of \(25^{\circ}\) with the positive \(x\)-axis, so using the component formulas:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}_{2} = \langle 52\cos(25^{\circ}), 52\sin(25^{\circ}) \rangle \approx \langle 47.128, 21.976 \rangle \end{equation*}
    The resultant force is the sum of the two forces:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}= \mathbf{F}_{1}+\mathbf{F}_{2} \approx \langle 45+47.128, 0+21.976 \rangle = \langle 92.128, 21.976 \rangle \end{equation*}
    Its magnitude is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf{F}| \approx \sqrt{92.128^{2}+21.976^{2}}= \sqrt{8487.568+482.945}= \sqrt{8970.513}\approx 94.71 \text{ lb} \end{equation*}
    Since both components of \(\mathbf{F}\) are positive, the resultant lies in the first quadrant, so its direction angle \(\theta\) from the positive \(x\)-axis satisfies
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \tan(\theta) \approx \frac{21.976}{92.128}\Rightarrow \theta \approx \arctan(0.2385) \approx 13.42^{\circ} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.1.27.

    The net force acting on an object is the sum of all individual forces acting on it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.28. Openstax, Calculus Volume 3, Section 2.1, Question 57.

    A 1500-lb boat is parked on a ramp that makes an angle of \(30^{\circ}\) with the horizontal. The boat’s weight vector points downward and is a sum of two vectors: a vector \(\mathbf{v}_{1}\) that is parallel to the ramp and a vector \(\mathbf{v}_{2}\) that is perpendicular to the inclined surface. The magnitudes of vectors \(\mathbf{v}_{1}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_{2}\) are the components of the boat’s weight vector parallel and perpendicular to the ramp, respectively. Find the magnitudes of \(\mathbf{v}_{1}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_{2}\text{.}\) (Round to the nearest integer.)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The weight vector \(\mathbf{w}\) points straight down and has magnitude \(|\mathbf{w}| = 1500\) lb. Since the ramp makes an angle of \(30^{\circ}\) with the horizontal, the direction perpendicular to the ramp makes that same angle of \(30^{\circ}\) with the vertical; that is, the angle between \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_{2}\) is \(30^{\circ}\text{,}\) as in the picture above. Decomposing \(\mathbf{w}\) into these two perpendicular components:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |\mathbf{v}_{1}| = 1500\sin(30^{\circ}) = 1500 \cdot \frac{1}{2}= 750 \text{ lb}\\ |\mathbf{v}_{2}| = 1500\cos(30^{\circ}) = 1500 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}= 750 \sqrt{3} \text{ lb} \end{equation*}
    As a check, \(\sqrt{750^{2}+1299^{2}}\approx 1500\text{,}\) the magnitude of the weight vector.
