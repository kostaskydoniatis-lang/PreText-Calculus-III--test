    Section 3.5 Tangent Planes and Local Linearization

    Note 3.5.1.

    Recall that given a function \(w=F(x, y, z)\text{,}\) we can intersect the function with the hyperplane \(w=c\) to get a 3-dimensional level surface \(c=F(x, y, z)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For example, the sphere \(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=9\) is a level surface of \(w=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\) when setting \(w=9\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.5.2.

    If a surface is given explicitly as \(z=f(x, y)\text{,}\) it can still be thought of as a level surface of a function \(w=F(x, y, z)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.5.3. Explicit and Level Surfaces.

    The surface \(z=f(x, y)\) is a level surface of \(w=z-f(x, y)\text{,}\) by setting \(w=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.5.4.

    Given \(f(x, y)=\frac{\sin x}{e^{xy}}\text{,}\) find a function \(w=F(x, y, z)\) such that \(f(x, y)\) is one of its level surfaces.
    🔗
    Solution.
    By the remark above, the surface \(z=f(x, y)\) is a level surface of \(w=z-f(x, y)\text{,}\) obtained by setting \(w=0\text{.}\) Hence we may take
    \begin{equation*} F(x, y, z) = z-\frac{\sin x}{e^{xy}} \end{equation*}
    and the graph of \(f(x, y)\) is exactly the level surface \(F(x, y, z)=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.5.5.

    We learned that a function \(z=f(x, y)\) is differentiable at \(P(a, b, f(a, b))\) if it looks like a plane when we zoom into the point \(P\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We will now give the equation that defines this plane and discuss where it comes from.
    🔗

    Definition 3.5.6. Tangent Plane to a Surface (Explicit).

    Given a differentiable surface \(z=f(x, y)\text{,}\) the tangent plane to the surface at the point ( \(a, b, f(a, b)\) ) is given by the equation
    \begin{equation*} z=f(a, b)+f_{x}(a, b)(x-a)+f_{y}(a, b)(y-b) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.5.7.

    Find an equation for the tangent plane to the surface \(1-0.5y^2-0.5x^2\) at the point \(\left(\sqrt2/2, \sqrt2/2, 0.5\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Here \(f(x, y)=1-0.5y^2-0.5x^2\) and \((a, b)=\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)\text{.}\) The partial derivatives are \(f_x = -x\) and \(f_y = -y\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} f_x\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \quad f_y\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \end{equation*}
    and \(f\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = 1-0.5\cdot\frac{1}{2}-0.5\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) which agrees with the given point. Substituting into the formula for the tangent plane:
    \begin{equation*} z=f(a, b)+f_{x}(a, b)(x-a)+f_{y}(a, b)(y-b) = \frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\left(x-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\left(y-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    \(f(x, y)= 1-0.5y^2-0.5x^2\) in green/blue and the tangent plane in grey
    🔗

    Note 3.5.8.

    The equation for the tangent plane depends on the point \((a, b, f(a, b))\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.5.9.

    Remember that every surface \(z=f(x, y)\) is a level surface of some function \(w=F(x, y, z)\text{.}\) Hence, to see where the equation for the tangent plane comes from, we work with (implicit) level surfaces of the form \(d=F(x, y, z)\text{,}\) where \(d\) is a scalar (not a variable).
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.5.10.

    If \(\vec{r}(t)\) is a 3D-curve on the surface \(d=F(x, y, z)\) passing through the point ( \(a, b, c\) ) on the surface at time \(t=t_{0}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \nabla F(a, b, c) \cdot \vec{r}^{\prime}\left(t_{0}\right)=0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This is true for EVERY differentiable curve on the surface passing through ( \(a, b, c\) ).
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗

    Remark 3.5.11. Gradient and Tangent Plane.

    Given a differentiable (level) surface \(d=F(x, y, z)\text{,}\) the tangent plane to the surface at the point ( \(a, b, c\) ) is the plane whose normal vector, given by \(\nabla F(a, b, c)\text{,}\) is orthogonal to the tangent vectors of every (differentiable) curve on the surface passing through ( \(a, b, c\) ).
    🔗
    🔗
    This observation gives rise to the following formula:
    🔗

    Definition 3.5.12. Tangent Plane to a Surface (Implicit).

    Given a differentiable surface \(d=F(x, y, z)\) and a point \((a, b, c)\) on the surface, the tangent plane to the surface at the point \((a, b, c)\) has normal vector \(\nabla F(a, b, c)\) and is given by the equation
    \begin{equation*} F_{x}(a, b, c)(x-a)+F_{y}(a, b, c)(y-b)+F_{z}(a, b, c)(z-c)=0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.5.13.

    Find an equation for the tangent plane to the surface \(x+ y^2 + z^3 = 1\) at the point \(\left( 0,0,1\right)\)
    🔗
    \(x+ y^2 + z^3 = 1\) in green and the tangent plane in light blue
    🔗
    Solution.
    The surface is the level surface \(F(x, y, z)=0\) of the function \(F(x, y, z)=x+y^{2}+z^{3}-1\text{,}\) and the point \((0,0,1)\) is indeed on the surface since \(0+0^2+1^3=1\text{.}\) The partial derivatives are \(F_x = 1\text{,}\) \(F_y = 2y\text{,}\) and \(F_z = 3z^2\text{,}\) so the normal vector to the tangent plane is
    \begin{equation*} \nabla F(0,0,1) = \left\langle 1, 2(0), 3(1)^2\right\rangle = \langle 1, 0, 3\rangle \end{equation*}
    Substituting into the formula for the (implicit) tangent plane:
    \begin{equation*} 1(x-0)+0(y-0)+3(z-1)=0 \Rightarrow x+3(z-1)=0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.5.14. Approximations.

    As we saw in Example 3.5.13, the equation \(x+ y^2 + z^3 = 1\) is more “complicated” than the equation for the tangent plane \(x+3(z-1)\) at \(\left( 0,0,1\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This is important because we can use the tangent plane \(x+3(z-1)\) to approximate \(x+ y^2 + z^3 = 1\) for points “close” to \((0,0,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.5.15. Tangent Planes and Approximations.

    Given a differentiable surface \(d=F(x, y, z)\text{,}\) its tangent plane at the point ( \(a, b, c\) ) can be used to approximate the surface for points close to ( \(a, b, c\) ).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.5.16.

    Given the surface \(f(x, y)=xye^{xy} \text{,}\) use tangent planes to approximate \(f(0.1,0.1)\) and \(f(0.1, 1.1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The idea is to choose, for each input, a nearby input \((a, b)\) at which \(f\) and its partial derivatives are easy to evaluate, and then approximate \(f\) by its tangent plane at \((a, b)\text{.}\) By the product and chain rules, the partial derivatives of \(f(x, y)=xye^{xy}\) are
    \begin{equation*} f_x = ye^{xy}+xye^{xy}\cdot y = ye^{xy}(1+xy), \quad f_y = xe^{xy}+xye^{xy}\cdot x = xe^{xy}(1+xy) \end{equation*}
    For \(f(0.1,0.1)\) the closest convenient input is \((a, b)=(0,0)\text{.}\) There \(f(0,0)=0\text{,}\) \(f_x(0,0)=0\text{,}\) and \(f_y(0,0)=0\text{,}\) so the tangent plane is
    \begin{equation*} z = 0+0(x-0)+0(y-0) = 0 \end{equation*}
    and hence \(f(0.1,0.1)\approx 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(f(0.1,1.1)\) the closest convenient input is \((a, b)=(0,1)\text{.}\) There \(f(0,1)=0\cdot 1\cdot e^{0}=0\text{,}\) \(f_x(0,1)=1\cdot e^{0}(1+0)=1\text{,}\) and \(f_y(0,1)=0\cdot e^{0}(1+0)=0\text{,}\) so the tangent plane is
    \begin{equation*} z = 0+1(x-0)+0(y-1) = x \end{equation*}
    and hence \(f(0.1,1.1)\approx 0.1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.5.17.

    Find all points on the surface \(z=x^{2}-2 x y-y^{2}- x+ y\) where the tangent plane is horizontal (parallel to the plane \(z=0\) ).
    🔗
    Solution.
    Writing the surface as \(z=f(x, y)\) with \(f(x, y)=x^{2}-2 x y-y^{2}- x+ y\text{,}\) the tangent plane at the point \((a, b, f(a, b))\) is
    \begin{equation*} z=f(a, b)+f_{x}(a, b)(x-a)+f_{y}(a, b)(y-b) \end{equation*}
    which is horizontal exactly when \(f_x(a, b)=0\) and \(f_y(a, b)=0\text{,}\) since then the equation reduces to the constant plane \(z=f(a, b)\text{.}\) The partial derivatives are
    \begin{equation*} f_x = 2x-2y-1, \quad f_y = -2x-2y+1 \end{equation*}
    Setting both equal to zero (\(2x-2y-1=0\) and \(-2x-2y+1=0\)) and adding the two equations gives
    \begin{equation*} (2x-2y-1)+(-2x-2y+1) = 0 \Rightarrow -4y = 0 \Rightarrow y=0 \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(y=0\) into \(f_x=0\) gives \(2x-1=0 \Rightarrow x=\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) and this also satisfies \(f_y = -2\cdot\frac{1}{2}-0+1 = 0\text{.}\) Finally
    \begin{equation*} f\left(\frac{1}{2}, 0\right) = \frac{1}{4}-0-0-\frac{1}{2}+0 = -\frac{1}{4} \end{equation*}
    So the only point where the tangent plane is horizontal is \(\left(\frac{1}{2}, 0, -\frac{1}{4}\right)\text{,}\) where the tangent plane is \(z=-\frac{1}{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.5.18. 3D gradient \(\nabla F(x, y, z)\) vs 2D gradient \(\nabla f(x, y)\).

    Do not confuse the 3D gradient \(\nabla F(x, y, z)\) with the 2D gradient \(\nabla f(x, y)\) we saw when studying directional derivatives. Here we summarize their different applications:
    🔗
    1. Given an (explicit) 3-dimensional surface \(z=f(x, y)\text{,}\) its 2 -dimensional gradient \(\nabla f(a, b)=\left\langle f_{x}(a, b), f_{y}(a, b)\right\rangle\) tells you the direction (in the \(x y\) plane) of steepest ascent at the input \(( a, b )\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Given an (implicit) 3-dimensional surface \(d=F(x, y, z)\text{,}\) its 3-dimensional gradient \(\nabla F(a, b, c)=\left\langle F_{x}(a, b, c), F_{y}(a, b, c), F_{z}(a, b, c)\right\rangle\) gives you a normal vector to the tangent plane to the surface at the point \((a, b, c)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.5.19.

    Given a differentiable function \(f(x, y)\text{,}\) we saw that we can approximate this function for inputs close to \((a, b)\) by using the tangent plane to \(f\) at the point ( \(a, b, f(a, b)\) ). That is,
    \begin{equation*} f(x, y) \approx f(a, b)+f_{x}(a, b)(x-a)+f_{y}(a, b)(y-b)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Since the expression on the right only involves constants and linear powers of \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) this approximation is referred to as the linearization of \(f(x, y)\) at the input \((a, b)\) .
    🔗

    Definition 3.5.20. Linearization of a Function at a Point.

    Given a differentiable function \(f(x, y)\text{,}\) its linearization (or linear approximation) at the input ( \(a, b\) ) is given by
    \begin{equation*} L(x, y)=f(a, b)+f_{x}(a, b)(x-a)+f_{y}(a, b)(y-b) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.5.21.

    Since the linearization of \(f\) at an input ( \(a, b\) ) is just the equation of the tangent plane to \(f\) at that input, the main application is the same: Approximate \(f\) with a “simpler” function.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.5.22.

    Find the linearization of \(f(x, y)=x^{3}-2x y+ y^2- y\) at the input \((1,2)\text{.}\) Use this to approximate \(f(0.9,2.1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We first compute the value of \(f\) and of its partial derivatives at the input \((1,2)\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} f(1,2) = 1^3 - 2(1)(2) + 2^2 - 2 = 1 - 4 + 4 - 2 = -1\\ f_x(x,y) = 3x^2 - 2y \Rightarrow f_x(1,2) = 3(1)^2 - 2(2) = 3 - 4 = -1\\ f_y(x,y) = -2x + 2y - 1 \Rightarrow f_y(1,2) = -2(1) + 2(2) - 1 = -2 + 4 - 1 = 1 \end{gather*}
    Substituting these into the formula for the linearization at \((1,2)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} L(x, y) = f(1,2) + f_x(1,2)(x-1) + f_y(1,2)(y-2) = -1 - (x-1) + (y-2) \end{equation*}
    Since the input \((0.9, 2.1)\) is close to \((1,2)\text{,}\) we can approximate \(f(0.9,2.1)\) by the linearization:
    \begin{equation*} f(0.9, 2.1) \approx L(0.9, 2.1) = -1-(0.9-1) + (2.1-2) = -1+0.1+0.1 = -0.8 \end{equation*}
    For comparison, the exact value is \(f(0.9,2.1) = (0.9)^3 - 2(0.9)(2.1) + (2.1)^2 - 2.1 = -0.741\text{,}\) so the approximation is fairly close.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.5.23.

    When talking about partial derivatives, we saw the follow formulas:
    \begin{equation*} f(a+\Delta x, b)-f(a, b) \approx f_{x}(a, b) \Delta x \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} f(a, b+\Delta y)-f(a, b) \approx f_{y}(a, b) \Delta y\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If we want to approximate a change in a function \(f(x, y)\) in both coordinates, we have a similar formula.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.5.24. Approximating Change.

    Given a differentiable function \(f(x, y)\text{,}\) we can approximate small changes in \(x\) and \(y\) using
    \begin{equation*} f(a+\Delta x, b+\Delta y)-f(a, b) \approx f_{x}(a, b) \Delta x+f_{y}(a, b) \Delta y\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Sometimes, this equation is written using the total differential of \(f\text{,}\) as
    \begin{equation*} d f=f_{x}(a, b) d x+f_{y}(a, b) d y \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.5.25.

    The volume of a right circular cone with radius \(r\) and height \(h\) is \(V=\pi r^{2} h / 3\text{.}\) Approximate the change in the volume of the cone when the radius changes from \(r=6\) to \(r=6.1\) and the height changes from \(h=4\) to \(h=3.9\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Here \(V(r,h) = \frac{\pi r^2 h}{3}\text{,}\) the radius changes from \(r=6\) by \(\Delta r = 0.1\) and the height changes from \(h=4\) by \(\Delta h = -0.1\text{.}\) We compute the partial derivatives of \(V\) at the input \((6,4)\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} V_r(r,h) = \frac{2\pi r h}{3} \Rightarrow V_r(6,4) = \frac{2\pi (6)(4)}{3} = 16\pi\\ V_h(r,h) = \frac{\pi r^2}{3} \Rightarrow V_h(6,4) = \frac{\pi (6)^2}{3} = 12\pi \end{gather*}
    By the approximating-change formula:
    \begin{gather*} V(6.1, 3.9) - V(6,4) \approx V_r(6,4) \Delta r + V_h(6,4) \Delta h = 16\pi (0.1) + 12\pi (-0.1)\\ = 1.6\pi - 1.2\pi = 0.4\pi \approx 1.26 \end{gather*}
    So the volume increases by approximately \(0.4\pi \approx 1.26\) cubic units. Even though the height decreases, the change coming from the radius is larger, so the net change in the volume is positive.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.5.26.

    This formula is useful when:
    🔗
    1. You do not have the explicit function, but you do know its rates of change.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The original function is too convoluted to work with, this approach can be more computationally efficient.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The formula for the linearization of a function generalizes easily to functions of more than two inputs.
    🔗

    Remark 3.5.27. Linearization in Higher Dimensions.

    Given a differentiable function \(w=f(x, y, z)\text{,}\) its linearization at the input ( \(a, b, c\) ) is given by
    \begin{equation*} L(x, y, z)=f(a, b, c)+f_{x}(a, b, c)(x-a)+f_{y}(a, b, c)(y-b)+f_{z}(a, b, c)(z-c)\text{.} \end{equation*}
