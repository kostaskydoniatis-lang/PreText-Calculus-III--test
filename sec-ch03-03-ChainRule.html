    Section 3.3 Multi-Variable Chain Rule

    Note 3.3.1.

    In this section we will consider two different cases when taking derivatives:
    🔗
    1. You have a function \(z=f(x, y)\) where \(x=x(t)\) and \(y=y(t)\) (both \(x\) and \(y\) are themselves functions of single variable \(t\) ).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. You have a function \(z=f(x, y)\) where \(x=x(s, t)\) and \(y=y(s, t)\) (both \(x\) and \(y\) are themselves functions of multiple variables \(s\) and \(t\) ).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.3.2. Chain Rule in Case 1.

    Given a function \(z=f(x, y)\) where \(x=x(t)\) and \(y=y(t)\text{,}\) then the derivative of \(f(x, y)\) with respect to \(t\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d f}{d t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \frac{d x}{d t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \frac{d y}{d t}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.3.3.

    In this case we can use the usual derivative notation since \(f(x, y)\) is a function of just the variable \(t\) if we replace \(x\) with \(x(t)\) and \(y\) with \(y(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.4.

    Find \(z^{\prime}(t)\text{,}\) where \(z=x^{3}-y, x=(t-1)^{2}, y=t^{3}+1\text{,}\) in the following ways:
    🔗
    (a) First make the substitutions \(x=x(t)\) and \(y=y(t)\text{,}\) and write \(z\) as a function of \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Using the Chain Rule.
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    Substituting \(x=(t-1)^2\) and \(y=t^3+1\) into \(z\) writes \(z\) as a function of just the variable \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} z(t) = \left( (t-1)^2 \right)^3 - (t^3+1) = (t-1)^6 - t^3 - 1 \end{equation*}
    Differentiating with respect to \(t\text{,}\) using the single-variable chain rule on \((t-1)^6\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} z'(t) = 6(t-1)^5 - 3t^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The partial derivatives of \(z\) and the derivatives of \(x\) and \(y\) are
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 3x^2, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -1, \quad \frac{dx}{dt} = 2(t-1), \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2 \end{equation*}
    so by the Chain Rule
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt} = 3x^2 \cdot 2(t-1) + (-1) \cdot 3t^2 \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(x=(t-1)^2\) to express everything in terms of \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} z'(t) = 3\left( (t-1)^2 \right)^2 \cdot 2(t-1) - 3t^2 = 6(t-1)^5 - 3t^2 \end{equation*}
    which agrees with the answer from part (a).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.3.5.

    The Chain Rule is an important tool because in many applications, you don’t know specific expressions for \(f(x, y), x(t)\) or \(y(t)\text{,}\) but do know the corresponding rates of change.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.6.

    Consider the Cobb-Douglas Production function \(P(L,K)\) where \(P\) represents production, \(K\) represents availability of capital, and \(L\) represents availability of labor. Suppose that both \(L\) and \(K\) are also functions of time \(t\) (which makes sense as the availability of capital and labor may change depending on time). Moreover, assume that
    🔗
    • \(L\) increases by 2 units when \(t\) increases 1 unit and \(P\) increases by 7 units when \(L\) increases 1 unit.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(K\) increases by 0.5 units when \(t\) increases 1 unit and \(P\) increases by 3 units when \(K\) increases 1 unit.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    When \(t\) increases by 1 unit, how many units does \(P\) increase by?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Here \(P\) depends on the intermediate variables \(L\) and \(K\text{,}\) and both are functions of \(t\text{,}\) so by the Chain Rule
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dP}{dt} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial L} \frac{dL}{dt} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial K} \frac{dK}{dt} \end{equation*}
    The given information is exactly these four rates of change: \(\frac{dL}{dt} = 2\) and \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial L} = 7\) from the first bullet point, and \(\frac{dK}{dt} = 0.5\) and \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial K} = 3\) from the second. Hence
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dP}{dt} = 7(2) + 3(0.5) = 14 + 1.5 = 15.5 \end{equation*}
    so when \(t\) increases by 1 unit, production \(P\) increases by approximately 15.5 units. Notice that we never needed explicit formulas for \(P(L,K)\text{,}\) \(L(t)\text{,}\) or \(K(t)\text{;}\) the rates of change were enough.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.3.7.

    The Chain Rule formula works no matter how many “intermediate variables” you have. For example, suppose \(w=f(x, y, z)\) and \(x=x(t), y=y(t)\text{,}\) and \(z=z(t)\) (here, \(x, y, z\) are your intermediate variables). Then
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d f}{d t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \frac{d x}{d t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \frac{d y}{d t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \frac{d z}{d t}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.3.8. Chain Rule in Case 2.

    Given a function \(z=f(x, y)\) where \(x=x(s, t)\) and \(y=y(s, t)\text{,}\) then the partial derivatives of \(f\) with respect to \(s\) and \(t\) have the following formulas:
    \begin{align*} \amp \frac{\partial f}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial s}\\ \amp \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.3.9.

    Here we must use the partial derivative notation since even if we substitute \(x=x(s, t)\) and \(y=y(s, t), f\) is still a function of two variables ( \(s\) and \(t\) ).
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.10.

    Let \(z=cos(x) \sin (y^2)\) where \(x=s+t\) and \(y=s-t\text{.}\) Compute \(z_{t}\) in the following ways:
    🔗
    (a) First make the substitutions \(x=x(s, t)\) and \(y=y(s, t)\text{,}\) and write \(z\) as a function of \(s\) and \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Using the Chain Rule.
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    Substituting \(x=s+t\) and \(y=s-t\) into \(z\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} z(s,t) = \cos(s+t) \sin\left( (s-t)^2 \right) \end{equation*}
    Differentiating with respect to \(t\) (treating \(s\) as a constant), using the product rule and the single-variable chain rule:
    \begin{gather*} z_t = -\sin(s+t) \sin\left( (s-t)^2 \right) + \cos(s+t) \cos\left( (s-t)^2 \right) \cdot 2(s-t)(-1)\\ = -\sin(s+t) \sin\left( (s-t)^2 \right) - 2(s-t) \cos(s+t) \cos\left( (s-t)^2 \right) \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The partial derivatives of \(z\text{,}\) \(x\text{,}\) and \(y\) are
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\sin(x) \sin(y^2), \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = 2y \cos(x) \cos(y^2), \quad \frac{\partial x}{\partial t} = 1, \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = -1 \end{equation*}
    so by the Chain Rule
    \begin{equation*} z_t = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = -\sin(x) \sin(y^2)(1) + 2y \cos(x) \cos(y^2)(-1) \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(x=s+t\) and \(y=s-t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} z_t = -\sin(s+t) \sin\left( (s-t)^2 \right) - 2(s-t) \cos(s+t) \cos\left( (s-t)^2 \right) \end{equation*}
    which agrees with the answer from part (a).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 3.3.11.

    This formula works no matter how many “intermediate variables” you have. For example, suppose \(w=f(x, y, z)\) and \(x=x(s, t), y=y(s, t)\text{,}\) and \(z=z(s, t)\) (here, \(x, y, z\) are your intermediate variables). Then
    \begin{align*} \amp \frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z} \frac{\partial z}{\partial s}\\ \amp \frac{\partial w}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial z} \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.3.12. Restricting the Domain of a Surface to a Curve.

    Given a surface \(z=f(x, y)\text{,}\) we sometimes are only interested in certain parts of the surface. Specifically, we can restrict the inputs of a surface to a curve in its domain by using appropriate substitutions \(x=x(t)\) and \(y=y(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.13.

    Suppose we are walking on the surface of a part of a sphere \(z=\sqrt{4-(x-1)^{2}-(y-2)^{2}}\) but we only want to walk above the line \(y= x+1\) in its domain. We can do this by substituting \(x=t\) and \(y= t+1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(y= x+1\) in purple and \(z(t, t+1)\) in yellow
    🔗
    Solution.
    Making the substitutions \(x=t\) and \(y=t+1\) restricts the inputs of the surface to the line \(y=x+1\text{,}\) and the height of the surface becomes a function of the single variable \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} z(t) = \sqrt{4 - (t-1)^2 - \left( (t+1)-2 \right)^2} = \sqrt{4 - (t-1)^2 - (t-1)^2} = \sqrt{4 - 2(t-1)^2} \end{equation*}
    This is the yellow curve in the picture. Note that for \(z(t)\) to be defined we need \(4 - 2(t-1)^2 \geq 0 \Rightarrow (t-1)^2 \leq 2 \Rightarrow 1-\sqrt{2} \leq t \leq 1+\sqrt{2}\text{;}\) these are exactly the values of \(t\) for which the line lies underneath the part of the sphere.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.14.

    Consider the surface of a part of a sphere \(z=\sqrt{4-(x-1)^{2}-(y-2)^{2}}\) and the curve \(C\) in the \(x y\)-plane given parametrically as \(x= \cos (t)+0.5\) and \(y= \sin (t)+1.5\text{,}\) where \(0 \leq t \leq 2 \pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Find \(z^{\prime}(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \((x-0.5)^2+(y-1.5)^2=1\) in purple and \(z( \cos (t)+0.5, \sin (t)+1.5)\) in yellow. blue
    🔗
    (b) Imagine that you are walking on the surface directly above the curve \(C\) (the surface restricted to inputs coming from \(C\) ). Find the values of \(t\) for which you are walking uphill ( \(z(t)\) is increasing).
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    We use the Chain Rule. Treating \(y\) and \(x\) as constants respectively, the partial derivatives of \(z\) are
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \frac{-(x-1)}{\sqrt{4-(x-1)^2-(y-2)^2}}, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{-(y-2)}{\sqrt{4-(x-1)^2-(y-2)^2}} \end{equation*}
    and the derivatives of the parametrization are \(\frac{dx}{dt} = -\sin(t)\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt} = \cos(t)\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} z'(t) = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{(x-1)\sin(t) - (y-2)\cos(t)}{\sqrt{4-(x-1)^2-(y-2)^2}} \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(x-1 = \cos(t)-0.5\) and \(y-2 = \sin(t)-0.5\text{,}\) the numerator becomes
    \begin{gather*} \left( \cos(t)-0.5 \right)\sin(t) - \left( \sin(t)-0.5 \right)\cos(t)\\ = \cos(t)\sin(t) - 0.5\sin(t) - \sin(t)\cos(t) + 0.5\cos(t) = 0.5\left( \cos(t) - \sin(t) \right) \end{gather*}
    and the expression under the square root becomes
    \begin{gather*} 4 - \left( \cos(t)-0.5 \right)^2 - \left( \sin(t)-0.5 \right)^2 = 4 - \left( \cos^2(t) + \sin^2(t) - \cos(t) - \sin(t) + 0.5 \right)\\ = 2.5 + \cos(t) + \sin(t) \end{gather*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} z'(t) = \frac{0.5\left( \cos(t) - \sin(t) \right)}{\sqrt{2.5 + \cos(t) + \sin(t)}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    We are walking uphill when \(z'(t) \gt 0\text{.}\) Since \(\cos(t)+\sin(t) \geq -2\text{,}\) the denominator satisfies \(\sqrt{2.5+\cos(t)+\sin(t)} \geq \sqrt{2.5-2} \gt 0\text{,}\) so the sign of \(z'(t)\) is the sign of the numerator \(0.5\left( \cos(t)-\sin(t) \right)\text{.}\) Another argument that arrives in the same conclusion is that denominator is always non-negative (as a square root) and well defined (not zero). Solving \(\cos(t) = \sin(t)\) on \([0, 2\pi]\) gives \(\tan(t) = 1 \Rightarrow t = \frac{\pi}{4}\) or \(t = \frac{5\pi}{4}\text{.}\) Testing a value in each of the resulting intervals: at \(t=0 \in [0,\frac{\pi}{4}]\) we get \(\cos(0)-\sin(0) = 1 \gt 0\text{;}\) at \(t=\pi\in [\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{5\pi}{4}]\) we get \(\cos(\pi)-\sin(\pi) = -1 \lt 0\text{;}\) and at \(t=\frac{3\pi}{2}\in [\frac{5\pi}{4},2\pi]\) we get \(\cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)-\sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}\right) = 0-(-1) = 1 \gt 0\text{.}\) Hence \(z(t)\) is increasing, and we are walking uphill, for
    \begin{equation*} 0 \leq t \lt \frac{\pi}{4} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{5\pi}{4} \lt t \leq 2\pi \end{equation*}
